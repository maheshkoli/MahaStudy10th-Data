Chapter at a Glance

Chapter 5, Mass Media and History, explains the meaning of mass media, the development of newspapers and other media in India, the importance of mass communication, the need to critically review media information, and professional opportunities that use historical knowledge.

The textbook is organised into five sections: Introduction to Mass Media; History of Mass Media; Why do we need Mass Media?; Critical understanding of information received through Mass Media; and Mass Media and Professional Opportunities.

5.1 Introduction to Mass Media

Mass means an aggregation of people, while media means different modes of communication. Mass media is therefore a field of mass communication that helps information reach many people, including people in distant places.

In earlier times, a crier announced important news in the streets by beating drums and calling out messages on the king’s orders. People then spread the news by word of mouth.

Newspapers

A newspaper is a publication that regularly prints news, editorials, people’s opinions, entertainment and supplementary content. It provides local, national and international news and serves as a historical document recording current events.

Precursors of newspapers

  • In ancient Egypt, inscriptions containing royal decrees were placed in public places.
  • Emperor Ashoka also used public inscriptions to communicate with his subjects.
  • In the Roman Empire, royal decrees were written and distributed in different regions.
  • During Julius Caesar’s reign, Acta Diurna (“acts of everyday”) were displayed in public places in Rome.
  • The textbook also mentions public distribution of royal dictates in the 7th century C.E., English hand-outs about wars and important events, travellers sharing stories, and royal ambassadors sending news to the court.

5.2 History of Mass Media

Newspapers in India

The textbook explains that printing technology and newspapers were introduced in India after the arrival of the British. Newspapers became an early mass medium for circulating information and knowledge.

  • Bengal Gazette: The first English newspaper in India, printed on 29 January 1780. It was also called the Calcutta General Advertiser and was started by James Augustus Hickey.
  • Darpan: Started in Mumbai in 1832; Balshastri Jambhekar was its editor. Its news helps readers study political, economic, social and cultural events of the time. The textbook calls Jambhekar the “First Editor” and notes that 6 January is observed in Maharashtra as Patrakar Din.
  • Prabhakar: Started by Bhau Mahajan. It published material including discussion of the French Revolution and the “Shatpatre” letters by Lokhitvadi (Gopal Hari Deshmukh).
  • Dnyanoday: Printed a map of Asia in 1842 and a map of Europe in 1851. It published news about the telegraph, the start of railways in India, and the 1857 uprising.
  • Induprakash: Supported widow remarriage.
  • Deenbandhu: Started by Krishnarao Bhalekar, a close associate of Mahatma Jyotirao Phule; it represented issues affecting Bahujansamaj.
  • Kesari and Maratha: Started in 1881 by Gopal Ganesh Agarkar and Bal Gangadhar Tilak. They discussed social and political issues and are described as an important stage in the history of Indian newspapers before independence.
  • Magazines and journals: Balshastri Jambhekar started the Marathi monthly magazine Digdarshan. The textbook also mentions the historical journal Pragati, started in 1929 and edited by Tryambak Shankar Shejwalkar.

Electronic and digital journalism

Web news portals, social media and online video channels make historical content available to audiences. The textbook also mentions the increasing use of e-newspapers.

Radio in India

  • The Indian Broadcasting Company (IBC) was a private radio company that broadcast daily programmes.
  • After being taken over by the British Government, it was renamed the Indian State Broadcasting Service (ISBS).
  • On 8 June 1936, it was renamed All India Radio (AIR).
  • After independence, AIR became part of the Ministry of Information and Broadcasting. The name “Akashvani” was suggested by poet Pandit Narendra Sharma.
  • The textbook notes that Vividh Bharati programmes were broadcast in 24 regional languages and 146 dialects.

Television in India

  • The Delhi Doordarshan Centre was inaugurated by Dr. Rajendra Prasad, the first President of India.
  • The Mumbai Doordarshan Centre began telecasting on 1 May 1972.
  • Colour television was introduced on 15 August 1982.
  • In 1991, the Indian government permitted private national and international channels to telecast in India.

5.3 Why Do We Need Mass Media?

  • Free flow of information: Helps information reach different sections of society.
  • Public opinion: Editorials, columns and supplements provide platforms for opinions.
  • Democracy: Newspapers can help strengthen democracy by informing citizens and allowing them to express views.
  • Audio-visual communication: Television combines sound and visuals, allowing viewers to see events in ways that print and radio cannot provide alone.
  • Access to events: Television can show visuals of events as they happen, which the textbook describes as an alternative for watching an event beyond the limitations of newspapers and radio.

5.4 Critical Understanding of Media Information

Information received through mass media must be reviewed critically because a report may not always represent the exact truth. Readers should examine the information carefully and consider whether it is authentic.

The “Hitler diaries” example in the textbook

The textbook describes how the German weekly magazine Stern purchased purported handwritten diaries of Adolf Hitler and sold them to publishing companies. Although the diaries were checked and reported in print, they were later proved to be forged. The example illustrates why media information must be verified before it is accepted or republished.

Study habit

  • Read the report carefully instead of accepting every claim immediately.
  • Check the authenticity of documents and the evidence supporting a claim.
  • Compare reports with reliable historical documents when possible.
  • Distinguish a reported claim from a verified fact.

The first two points reflect the textbook’s central lesson about careful review and authenticity; the latter two are practical study suggestions derived from that lesson.

5.5 Mass Media and Professional Opportunities

Historical knowledge helps journalists and media professionals explain the background of current events. Reporters compare present events with similar events from the past, and newspapers may publish historical information in special frames, columns, reviews, articles and editorials.

Where history is used in media

  • Newspapers: Historical columns such as “50 Years Ago,” “100 Years Ago” and “On This Day in History”; special supplements; reviews and articles on anniversaries.
  • Radio: Akashvani preserves recordings of Independence Day speeches by prime ministers, invites historians for discussions, and broadcasts programmes such as “On This Day in History.”
  • Television: Historical and mythological serials and documentary channels introduce audiences to past periods. The textbook mentions Ramayana, Mahabharata, Bharat Ek Khoj and Raja Shivchhatrapati, as well as Discovery, National Geographic and History channels.
  • Historical production accuracy: Historical programmes need careful research into the period’s surroundings, clothing, weapons, lifestyles and language.

Bharat Ek Khoj

The textbook describes Bharat Ek Khoj as a significant Doordarshan serial based on Jawaharlal Nehru’s book The Discovery of India, directed by Shyam Benegal. It presented Indian history from ancient to modern times and covered political, social and cultural developments. Roshan Seth played Nehru and also narrated parts of the programme.

2. Write brief notes

(1) The role of newspapers in the Indian struggle for independence:
Newspapers circulated political and social ideas, reported events and helped create public awareness. The textbook describes Darpan, Prabhakar, Dnyanoday, Deenbandhu, Kesari and Maratha as sources for understanding the issues and movements of their times. Kesari and Maratha discussed social and political issues before independence.

(2) Why do we need mass media?
Mass media enables information to reach different sections of society. Newspapers provide news and a platform for opinions through editorials and columns. Media helps people understand events, supports public discussion and can strengthen democracy. Television adds visuals to sound and information.

(3) Mass media and professional opportunities:
Historical knowledge supports journalism, newspaper columns, editorials, special supplements, radio discussions and historical television programmes. Professionals need to research the relevant period carefully so that details such as clothing, weapons, lifestyles and language are represented accurately.

3. Explain the statements with reasons

(1) Any information received through mass media needs to be reviewed critically.
Media reports may not always be true. The textbook’s example of the purported Hitler diaries shows that material can be published after checks and later be proved forged. Therefore, authenticity should be examined carefully.

(2) Knowledge of history is essential for newspaper articles.
Historical knowledge helps reporters explain the background of current events, compare them with similar past events, and prepare columns, reviews and special supplements about historical anniversaries.

(3) Television is the most popular medium.
The chapter explains that television combines audio and visuals and allows people to see events and programmes. The textbook presents its audio-visual nature as an important advantage over newspapers and radio; it does not provide audience statistics proving a universal popularity ranking.

4. Answer in 25–30 words

(1) Explain the objectives of newspapers.
Newspapers regularly publish local, national and international news, editorials, public opinions and supplementary content. They inform readers, record current events and provide material for historical study.

(2) How is history helpful in planning Akashvani programmes?
History helps Akashvani prepare programmes about national leaders and historical events. Historians contribute to discussions, preserved speeches provide records, and “On This Day in History” shares relevant historical information.
