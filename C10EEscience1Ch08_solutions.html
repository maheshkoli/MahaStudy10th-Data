1. Chapter at a Glance

Metallurgy is the science and technology of extracting metals from ores and purifying them for use. The chapter first compares the physical and chemical properties of metals and nonmetals, then explains the reactivity series, ionic compounds, concentration of ores, extraction according to reactivity, refining and corrosion prevention.

Ore

A mineral from which a metal can be separated economically.

Gangue

Unwanted soil, sand and rocky impurities present with an ore.

Concentration

Separating gangue from the ore to increase the proportion of the desired metal compound.

Metallurgy

The extraction of metals from ores and their purification.

3. Chemical Properties of Metals and the Reactivity Series

Metals tend to lose electrons and form positively charged ions (cations); for this reason the textbook calls them electropositive elements. Nonmetals tend to accept electrons and form negatively charged ions (anions).

Metals reacting with oxygen

4Na + O₂ → 2Na₂O
Na₂O + H₂O → 2NaOH
2Mg + O₂ → 2MgO
MgO + H₂O → Mg(OH)₂

The textbook notes that sodium is stored in kerosene to prevent contact with air and moisture.

Metals reacting with water

2Na + 2H₂O → 2NaOH + H₂ + heat
2K + 2H₂O → 2KOH + H₂ + heat
Ca + 2H₂O → Ca(OH)₂ + H₂

Sodium and potassium react rapidly with water; calcium reacts more slowly. Aluminium, iron and zinc react with steam to form oxides and hydrogen, according to the textbook:

2Al + 3H₂O(g) → Al₂O₃ + 3H₂
3Fe + 4H₂O(g) → Fe₃O₄ + 4H₂
Zn + H₂O(g) → ZnO + H₂

Metals reacting with dilute acids

Magnesium, aluminium, zinc and iron react with dilute hydrochloric or sulphuric acid to form a salt and hydrogen gas. The chapter gives this relative order for these metals:

Mg > Al > Zn > Fe
Mg + 2HCl → MgCl₂ + H₂
2Al + 6HCl → 2AlCl₃ + 3H₂
Fe + 2HCl → FeCl₂ + H₂

The source also explains that nitric acid forms metal nitrates and nitrogen oxides; the products depend on the acid concentration.

Displacement reactions

If metal A displaces metal B from B's salt solution, A is more reactive than B. Iron displaces copper from copper sulphate, so iron is more reactive than copper.

Fe + CuSO₄ → FeSO₄ + Cu

Reactivity series shown in the textbook

K > Na > Li > Ca > Mg > Al > Zn > Fe > Sn > Pb > Cu > Hg > Ag > Au
  • Highly reactive: potassium, sodium, lithium, calcium.
  • Moderately reactive: magnesium, aluminium, zinc, iron, tin, lead.
  • Less reactive: copper, mercury, silver, gold.

Chemical properties of nonmetals

  • With oxygen, nonmetals generally form acidic oxides; some form neutral oxides.
  • Most nonmetals do not react with water; the textbook gives chlorine as an exception.
  • Most nonmetals do not react with dilute acids; the textbook describes the reaction of chlorine with hydrobromic acid.
  • Under suitable conditions, nonmetals react with hydrogen; examples in the chapter include H₂S and NH₃.
C + O₂ → CO₂
2C + O₂ → 2CO
S + O₂ → SO₂
Cl₂ + H₂O → HOCl + HCl
Cl₂ + 2HBr → 2HCl + Br₂

4. Ionic Compounds

Ionic compounds form from positively charged cations and negatively charged anions. The electrostatic attraction between oppositely charged ions is called an ionic bond. The charges balance, so the compound is electrically neutral.

Properties of ionic compounds

  • Usually crystalline, hard and brittle solids.
  • High melting and boiling points because strong attraction between ions requires substantial energy to overcome.
  • Many are soluble in water but insoluble in solvents such as kerosene and petrol, as described in the textbook.
  • Do not conduct electricity in the solid state because ions cannot move freely.
  • Conduct electricity when molten or dissolved in water because ions become mobile; such compounds are called electrolytes in these states.

5. Metallurgy, Minerals, Ores and Gangue

  • Minerals: Naturally occurring compounds of metals found with impurities.
  • Ores: Minerals from which the metal can be separated economically.
  • Gangue: Soil, sand, rocky substances and other unwanted impurities present in ore.
  • Metallurgy: The process of extracting metals from ores and purifying them.

The broad stages described in the chapter are concentration of the ore, extraction of the metal and purification/refining.

6. Methods of Concentration of Ores

Gravitational separation

Separates heavier ore particles from lighter gangue particles using differences in weight/density.

  • Wilfley table method: Powdered ore and water move over a vibrating, inclined table with riffles. Lighter gangue is carried away by water while heavier ore particles collect between the riffles.
  • Hydraulic separation: A water jet carries lighter gangue out of the upper outlet while heavier ore particles settle at the bottom.

Magnetic separation

Uses a conveyor belt and magnetic roller to separate magnetic ingredients from nonmagnetic ingredients. The textbook gives cassiterite as an example: nonmagnetic stannic oxide (SnO₂) is separated from magnetic ferrous tungstate (FeWO₄).

Froth flotation

Based on different wetting properties. Sulphide ore particles are preferentially wetted by oil, while gangue is wetted by water. Air is passed through a mixture of powdered ore, water and oil; the froth carries sulphide ore to the surface. The textbook mentions zinc blende (ZnS) and copper pyrite (CuFeS₂).

Leaching

The ore is soaked in a suitable solution that dissolves the desired compound while gangue remains undissolved. The textbook describes leaching bauxite with aqueous sodium hydroxide or sodium carbonate.

7. Extraction of Metals

Highly reactive metals

Potassium, sodium, calcium, magnesium and aluminium are high in the reactivity series. The textbook explains that reactive metals are extracted by electrolytic reduction. For molten sodium chloride:

Cathode: Na⁺ + e⁻ → Na (reduction)
Anode: 2Cl⁻ → Cl₂ + 2e⁻ (oxidation)

Extraction of aluminium from bauxite

Bauxite is represented as Al₂O₃·nH₂O and contains alumina together with impurities such as silica, ferric oxide and titanium oxide.

  1. Concentration: Bayer’s process uses concentrated sodium hydroxide; Hall’s process uses aqueous sodium carbonate. The soluble sodium aluminate is separated from insoluble impurities.
  2. Precipitation and calcination: Aluminium hydroxide is precipitated, filtered, washed, dried and heated to obtain alumina.
  3. Electrolytic reduction: Alumina is electrolysed in a molten mixture. Cryolite (Na₃AlF₆) and fluorspar (CaF₂) lower the melting point from above 2000 °C to about 1000 °C. The graphite lining acts as cathode and graphite rods act as anodes. Aluminium collects at the bottom; oxygen is released at the anode and reacts with the carbon anodes, so they need replacement.
2Al(OH)₃ → Al₂O₃ + 3H₂O
Cathode: Al³⁺ + 3e⁻ → Al
Anode: 2O²⁻ → O₂ + 4e⁻

Moderately reactive metals

The chapter describes metals such as iron, zinc, lead and copper as moderately reactive. Sulphide ores are generally converted to oxides by roasting (heating strongly in air); carbonate ores are converted to oxides by calcination (heating in limited air). The oxides are then reduced to obtain the metal.

Less reactive metals

The textbook discusses less reactive metals lower in the reactivity series and gives examples of obtaining metals from their ores by heating or other suitable reduction processes.

Do not confuse: Roasting is associated with sulphide ores and air; calcination is associated with carbonate ores and limited air.

8. Refining, Corrosion and Prevention

Corrosion is the gradual damage of a metal due to reactions with its surroundings. The chapter describes ways to protect metals by preventing contact with air/moisture or by applying protective coatings.

  • Galvanizing: Coating iron or steel with zinc.
  • Tinning: Applying a layer of tin.
  • Anodization: Producing a thicker protective oxide layer on aluminium using electrolysis.
  • Electroplating: Depositing a layer of one metal on another by electrolysis.
  • Alloying: Mixing a metal with other elements in suitable proportions to obtain desired properties; examples in the chapter include stainless steel and bronze.

Amalgam: An alloy in which one of the metals is mercury.

Q5. Give scientific reasons.

a. Lemon or tamarind is used to clean copper vessels that have turned greenish. The acids in lemon or tamarind react with the green corrosion layer on the copper vessel and help remove it.

b. Ionic compounds generally have high melting points. Strong electrostatic attraction between oppositely charged ions requires a large amount of energy to overcome.

c. Sodium is stored in kerosene. Sodium reacts readily with oxygen and moisture and may catch fire; kerosene prevents direct contact with air and water.

d. Pine oil is used in froth flotation. It helps form froth; sulphide ore particles preferentially become wetted by oil and rise with the froth, while gangue remains wetted by water.

e. Anodes need replacement during electrolysis of alumina. Oxygen released at the graphite anode reacts with its carbon to form carbon dioxide, gradually consuming the anode.

Q6. A copper coin is dipped in silver nitrate solution. Why does a glitter appear? Write the equation.

Copper is more reactive than silver and displaces silver from silver nitrate. The deposited silver gives the coin a glittering appearance.

Cu + 2AgNO₃ → Cu(NO₃)₂ + 2Ag

Q7. Metal A has electronic configuration 2,8,1 and metal B has 2,8,2. Which is more reactive? Give reactions with dilute hydrochloric acid.

Following the textbook solution’s intended identification: A is sodium and B is magnesium. Sodium is more reactive than magnesium because it loses its single outermost electron readily.

2Na + 2HCl → 2NaCl + H₂
Mg + 2HCl → MgCl₂ + H₂

These are the textbook’s expected equations; sodium reacts dangerously vigorously with water and should only be handled in a properly supervised laboratory.

10. Quick Revision Sheet

  • Metals lose electrons and form cations; nonmetals generally gain electrons and form anions.
  • Reactivity series: K, Na, Li, Ca, Mg, Al, Zn, Fe, Sn, Pb, Cu, Hg, Ag, Au (in decreasing reactivity, with the textbook's sequence).
  • A more reactive metal displaces a less reactive metal from its salt solution.
  • Ore = economically useful mineral; gangue = unwanted impurities.
  • Concentration methods: gravitational separation, magnetic separation, froth flotation and leaching.
  • Roasting: heating sulphide ores in air. Calcination: heating carbonate ores in limited air.
  • Highly reactive metals are extracted by electrolytic reduction.
  • Ionic compounds are generally hard, brittle, crystalline and have high melting points; they conduct when molten or dissolved, not when solid.
  • Galvanizing uses zinc; tinning uses tin; anodization thickens the oxide layer; electroplating deposits a metal coating.
Exam tip: Learn the reactivity series, ore-concentration methods, key definitions and balanced chemical equations. In displacement questions, identify which metal is higher in the reactivity series before predicting the products.
