Chapter 2: Periodic Classification of Elements

1. Chapter at a Glance

This chapter explores the historical development of the periodic table, starting from early attempts to classify elements based on their properties. It covers:

  • Dobereiner’s Triads: Grouping elements with similar properties into sets of three.
  • Newlands’ Law of Octaves: Arranging elements by increasing atomic mass and observing the repetition of properties every eighth element.
  • Mendeleev’s Periodic Table: The first systematic arrangement based on atomic mass and chemical properties, which predicted the existence of undiscovered elements.
  • Modern Periodic Law: The current classification based on atomic numbers, which resolved many limitations of earlier tables.
  • Periodic Trends: How properties like valency, atomic size, and metallic/non-metallic character vary across periods and down groups.

2. Important Concepts Explained

Classification of Elements

  • Early Classification: Elements were initially classified simply as metals and non-metals. Later, metalloids were identified.
  • Dobereiner’s Triads: Dobereiner arranged elements in groups of three (triads) with similar chemical properties in increasing order of atomic mass. The atomic mass of the middle element was approximately the mean of the other two.
  • Example: Li (6.9), Na (23.0), K (39.1). Mean of Li and K = (6.9 + 39.1) / 2 = 23.0.

Newlands’ Law of Octaves

  • Newlands arranged elements in increasing order of atomic mass. He observed that every eighth element had properties similar to the first, comparing this to the seven notes of music (Saptak).
  • Limitations: It was only applicable up to calcium. It did not account for newly discovered elements, and some elements with different properties were placed together.

Mendeleev’s Periodic Table

  • Mendeleev arranged 63 elements in increasing order of atomic mass.
  • Periodic Law: "Properties of elements are periodic function of their atomic masses."
  • Merits: He left vacant spaces for undiscovered elements (e.g., eka-boron, eka-aluminium, eka-silicon) and correctly predicted their properties. He also created a 'zero' group for noble gases.
  • Demerits: Ambiguity in the position of cobalt and nickel (same atomic mass), the challenge of placing isotopes, and the non-uniform rise in atomic mass.

Modern Periodic Table

  • Modern Periodic Law: "Properties of elements are a periodic function of their atomic numbers."
  • Structure: Contains 7 horizontal rows (periods) and 18 vertical columns (groups).
  • Blocks: Divided into s-block, p-block, d-block (transition elements), and f-block (lanthanide and actinide series).
  • Electronic Configuration: The number of valence electrons determines the group, and the number of shells determines the period.

3. Definitions and Key Terms

  • Triad: A group of three elements with similar chemical properties where the middle element's atomic mass is the average of the other two.
  • Octave: A pattern where properties repeat every eighth element.
  • Period: The horizontal rows in the periodic table.
  • Group: The vertical columns in the periodic table.
  • Atomic Number (Z): The number of protons in the nucleus of an atom.
  • Valence Electrons: Electrons present in the outermost shell of an atom.
  • Valency: The combining capacity of an element, determined by the number of valence electrons.
  • Atomic Radius: The distance between the nucleus of an atom and its outermost shell.
  • Electropositivity (Metallic Character): The tendency of an atom to lose valence electrons to form a cation.
  • Electronegativity (Non-metallic Character): The tendency of an atom to accept electrons to form an anion.

  • Periodic Trends:

  • Down a group (top to bottom): Atomic radius increases, electronegativity decreases, electropositivity increases.

7. Important Questions and Answers

Q1: Why did Mendeleev leave gaps in his periodic table?

  • A: He left gaps for elements that were not yet discovered. He predicted their properties based on the properties of their neighbors, which helped in their later discovery.

Q2: How does atomic size change in a period?

  • A: Atomic size decreases from left to right in a period because the nuclear charge increases, pulling the electrons closer to the nucleus.

Q3: Why are noble gases placed in a separate group?

  • A: Noble gases are chemically inert and have a complete octet (or duplet for Helium). They do not react with other elements, so they are placed in a separate 'zero' group.

8. Common Errors and How to Avoid Them

  • Confusing Atomic Mass and Atomic Number: Remember that Mendeleev used atomic mass, while the Modern Periodic Table uses atomic number.
  • Miscounting Shells: Ensure you count all shells (K, L, M, etc.) to determine the period correctly.
  • Valency vs. Valence Electrons: Valence electrons are the electrons in the outermost shell. Valency is the capacity to combine, which may be equal to the number of valence electrons or 8 minus the number of valence electrons.

6. Short notes

a. Mendeleev’s periodic law: The properties of elements are periodic functions of their atomic masses. Mendeleev arranged known elements by increasing atomic mass and placed elements with similar properties in the same groups.

b. Structure of the modern periodic table: Elements are arranged by increasing atomic number. The table has 7 periods (horizontal rows) and 18 groups (vertical columns), divided into s-, p-, d- and f-blocks. Lanthanides and actinides are shown separately at the bottom.

c. Position of isotopes: In Mendeleev’s table, isotopes caused difficulty because they have different atomic masses but similar chemical properties. In the modern table, isotopes occupy the same position because they have the same atomic number.

7. Give scientific reasons

  1. Atomic radius decreases across a period: Nuclear charge increases while added electrons enter the same shell, so the nucleus attracts them more strongly.
  2. Metallic character decreases across a period: Effective nuclear attraction increases and atoms hold valence electrons more strongly, making electron loss more difficult.
  3. Atomic radius increases down a group: Each successive element has an additional occupied shell, increasing the distance of the outermost shell from the nucleus.
  4. Elements in the same group have the same valency: They generally have the same number of valence electrons, so their combining capacity is similar.
  5. Period 3 contains only eight elements although the third shell can hold 18: In this period, the 3s and 3p subshells fill, giving eight elements; the 3d subshell begins filling in the next period.
