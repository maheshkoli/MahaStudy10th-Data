1. Chapter at a Glance

Main focus

This chapter explains the importance of tourism, transport and communication in the development of India and Brazil. It compares tourist arrivals, transport networks, communication systems and standard time.

Core idea

Natural features, relief, drainage, forests, population distribution and economic development influence where transport and communication networks grow. Transport development is described in the textbook as an indicator of a country's progress.

2. Key Terms and Definitions

Tourism

Travel to places for leisure, heritage, adventure, culture, health, business or other purposes. The chapter treats tourism as an important economic activity.

Ecotourism

Tourism focused on natural environments and sensitive ecosystems, with attention to appreciating and protecting them.

Invisible trade

The textbook classifies tourism as invisible trade because it mainly involves services rather than the physical exchange of goods.

Transport

The movement of people and goods from one place to another by road, rail, water or air.

Communication

The exchange of information through means such as landlines, mobile phones, television, radio, the internet and satellites.

GMT

Greenwich Mean Time, used in the textbook as the reference for comparing time zones.

IST

Indian Standard Time. The textbook identifies 82.5° E longitude as the standard meridian and states that IST is 5 hours 30 minutes ahead of GMT.

BRT

Brasilia Time. The textbook states that the official Brazilian time shown in its map is GMT−03, or three hours behind GMT.

Standard time

A common official time adopted for a region or country so that transport, communication and public activities can be coordinated.

4. Transport in Brazil

Roadways

The textbook says roadways are Brazil's most common transport method and account for more than half of the transport system. Road density is concentrated in eastern Brazil.

Why roads are less developed in parts of the Amazon basin

Dense forests in the Amazon River basin and swampy land have limited road development in those areas.

Waterways

Commercial waterways have developed along the Amazon River. The textbook mentions navigation for about 3,700 km and also identifies the south-flowing Parana River as important for waterways. Coastal shipping is also used.

Railways

The chapter says railways have not developed very well in Brazil. Long-distance passenger trains are restricted largely to a few urban tourist routes, even though trains can be cheaper.

Airways

According to the textbook, airways make a smaller contribution to Brazil's transport system than road transport.

Why railways developed in south-eastern Brazil

A textbook-based explanation is that this region contains major cities and economic activities that create demand for transport. The chapter's map should be used to support the specific location-based answer.

Why transport is sparse in some Brazilian regions

The textbook specifically identifies dense forests and swampy lands in the Amazon basin as obstacles to road development. Inaccessibility and physical geography also make network expansion difficult.

5. Transport in India

Overall network

The chapter states that India has a denser transport network than Brazil.

Road transport

According to the textbook, roads carry about 85% of passenger traffic and 70% of freight traffic.

Railways

Railways are important for India's economy and for moving people and freight over long distances. The textbook says the network is dense in the North Indian Plains and less dense in hill states, north-eastern states, central India and Rajasthan.

Waterways and sea routes

The chapter describes waterways as a relatively cheap means of transport, including rivers, canals, backwaters and creeks. It states that approximately 95% of India's foreign trade moves through ocean routes; this is a textbook figure and should be understood in that context.

Airways

The textbook says India's airways are more developed than Brazil's and that domestic air travel is increasing.

Figures 9.10 and 9.11

The maps on pages 64–65 show India's national highways and major ports, railway routes, important railway stations and airports. Use the map labels to name locations and compare network density.

Why the North Indian Plains have a dense railway network

The chapter asks students to relate railway density to geography. Flat plains make railway construction easier and the region's population and economic activity create substantial transport demand.

Why transport is sparse in some areas

The textbook identifies hill states, north-eastern states, central parts of India and Rajasthan as areas with relatively less dense railway networks.

6. Communication in Brazil

Telecommunication services

The chapter describes Brazil's landline and mobile services, television and radio broadcasting, and computer/internet access as fairly modern, especially in central-southern Brazil.

Uneven development

The textbook says northern and north-western Brazil are much less developed in telecommunications.

Reasons for limited coverage

Brazil's large land area and areas that are sparsely populated or densely vegetated create difficulties in extending telecommunications coverage.

Mobile phones and satellites

The chapter says mobile phones have driven rapid expansion of telecommunications. It also discusses Brazil's development of technology for launching domestically made satellites with its own rockets.

Brazilian Space Agency

The textbook identifies the Brazilian Space Agency (AEB) as the civilian authority responsible for the country's space programme.

7. Communication in India

Growth of communication

The chapter says electronic media, mobile phones, the internet and satellites have helped expand communication in India.

ISRO

The textbook identifies the Indian Space Research Organisation (ISRO) as responsible for India's space-launch programmes and notes India's progress in satellite launches.

Comparison with Brazil

The supplied chapter describes India's communication sector as rapidly growing and notes that Brazil's telecommunications are more developed in central-southern areas than in its north and north-west. Avoid assuming the two countries have identical patterns.

8. Time Zones and Standard Time

Longitude-time rule

The Earth rotates 360° in 24 hours. Therefore, 15° corresponds to 1 hour and 1° corresponds to 4 minutes of local time.

Formula

Time difference in minutes = difference in longitude (in degrees) × 4. Convert minutes of arc to fractions of a degree before calculating.

Brazil

The textbook describes four time zones, with offsets of 2, 3, 4 or 5 hours behind GMT. It identifies GMT−03 as the official Brazilian time shown in Figure 9.12.

Brazil's longitudinal extent

The textbook states that the time difference between the westernmost and easternmost points is about 168 minutes (2 hours 48 minutes). Note: the printed coordinates on page 66 are approximately 70°59′ W and 34°47′ W; subtracting these gives 36°12′, which converts to about 145 minutes. The textbook's stated result and the printed coordinates do not agree, so verify this item with the teacher or original map before using it in a calculation.

India

The textbook states that India has one standard time zone. The standard meridian is 82.5° E, passing through the Allahabad/Prayagraj region as named in the text, and IST is 5 hours 30 minutes ahead of GMT.

India's longitudinal time difference

The chapter states that the difference between India's two extreme points is about 120 minutes (2 hours), even though India follows one standard time for the whole country.

Local time calculation example

If it is 12 noon at Delhi (IST, GMT+5:30), then using the textbook's Brasilia time (GMT−3), Brasilia is 8 hours 30 minutes behind Delhi. So the corresponding time in Brasilia is 3:30 a.m. on the same date.

Why standard time matters

A single standard time helps coordinate railway and air schedules, communication, administration and other activities across a country.

10. Worked Examples

Example 1 — Convert longitude to time

A difference of 10° of longitude corresponds to 10 × 4 = 40 minutes of local time.

Example 2 — Compare IST and Brasilia time

IST = GMT+5:30; Brasilia time in the chapter = GMT−3. Difference = 5 hours 30 minutes + 3 hours = 8 hours 30 minutes. If Delhi is at 12:00 noon, Brasilia is at 3:30 a.m. on the same date.

Example 3 — Balance time calculation

When comparing two places, first establish which is east and which is west. Eastern longitudes have later local solar time than western longitudes. Then apply the 4-minutes-per-degree rule and adjust the date if the calculation crosses midnight.

Map-reading method

Read the title and legend, identify the symbols for roads, railways, ports and airports, and then describe where the network is dense or sparse. Use only places and routes shown on the map.
