1. Chapter at a Glance

Focus of the chapter

This chapter compares the climate of Brazil and India. It explains the influence of latitude, altitude, winds, mountain barriers and proximity to the sea. Figures 4.1–4.5 use maps and climate graphs to compare temperature and rainfall.

Brazil

Brazil receives rainfall from the South-East Trade Winds and North-East Trade Winds. The Amazon Valley is hot and wet; highlands are cooler; coastal areas are generally mild and humid. The Brazilian Highlands obstruct winds from the sea and cause orographic rainfall on the coastal side. The area beyond the highlands receives much less rainfall and is called the Drought Quadrilateral.

India

India has a monsoon-type climate. The Himalayas block very cold winds from the north. In summer, low pressure develops over the Punjab Plains and Thar Desert, attracting moisture-laden winds from the Indian Ocean. India has four seasons in the textbook’s classification: hot weather, monsoon, retreating monsoon and cold weather.

2. Brazil — Climate

Latitudinal extent

The equator passes through the northern part of Brazil. The northern region is hotter, while temperatures are comparatively lower in the southern part. The textbook notes that temperate-type conditions occur near the Tropic of Capricorn.

Trade winds and rainfall

Brazil receives rainfall from the South-East Trade Winds and the North-East Trade Winds. Parts of the Brazilian Highlands extend towards the northern coast. The highlands obstruct winds coming from the sea, causing orographic rainfall in coastal regions.

Drought Quadrilateral

Beyond the highlands, the effect of moisture-bearing winds is reduced, so rainfall is minimal. The textbook calls this rain-shadow area the Drought Quadrilateral. The northeastern part of the Brazilian Highlands is therefore much drier than the windward coastal region.

Temperature

The textbook gives the Amazon Valley’s average temperature as about 25°C–28°C. The highlands are cooler. Coastal regions experience a mild and humid climate because of their proximity to the sea. Seasonal temperature variation near the equatorial Brazilian coast is small.

Tropical cyclones

The textbook explains that tropical cyclones rarely visit Brazil’s coast because the convergence zone of the trade winds is weak near the equator. Winds in this region may move vertically, and cyclones are not formed frequently.

Snowfall

Brazil is mainly a tropical country and normally does not receive snowfall. The textbook says snowfall has occurred exceptionally in the south when southern polar air masses reach the region; it cites 1879, 1957 and 1985.

3. India — Climate

Monsoon-type climate

The textbook describes India’s climate as monsoon type. The Tropic of Cancer passes through the middle of the country. Sunrays are more direct in the tropical region, contributing to generally high temperatures.

Role of the Himalayas

The Himalayas obstruct very cold winds blowing from the north. They also influence the origin and movement of monsoon winds and cause orographic rainfall where moisture-laden winds meet the mountains.

Role of the Indian Ocean

In summer, low-pressure areas develop over the Punjab Plains and Thar Desert. These attract moisture-laden winds from the high-pressure region over the Indian Ocean, bringing rainfall to the mainland.

Rainfall distribution

Rainfall is higher on windward coastal slopes where the Western and Eastern Ghats obstruct moist winds. Rainfall decreases on the leeward sides. The monsoon winds blow parallel to the Aravalis, so parts of Gujarat and Rajasthan receive little rainfall.

Retreating monsoon

After reaching the Himalayan region, the monsoon winds retreat. Winds blowing from the north-east towards the Indian Ocean bring rainfall to some parts of the Peninsula. Tamil Nadu receives maximum rainfall during the retreating monsoon season, according to the textbook.

Four seasons

The textbook lists: (1) hot weather season, (2) season of rainfall or monsoon, (3) retreating monsoon season and (4) cold weather season.

Temperature variation

Temperatures generally decrease from south to north and with increasing altitude. The textbook notes that winter temperatures can fall to around −40°C in Jammu and Kashmir and parts of the Himalayan mountains. It also notes that Ganganagar in Rajasthan can reach 50°C in June and Kargil can experience temperatures as low as −48°C in winter.

4. Key Terms

Climate

The usual pattern of weather conditions of a place over a long period.

Isotherms

Lines on a map joining places that have the same temperature.

Rain-shadow region

An area receiving relatively little rainfall because hills or mountains block rain-bearing winds.

Orographic rainfall

Rainfall caused when moist air is forced to rise over hills or mountains, cools and condenses.

Trade winds

Large-scale winds that blow towards the equatorial region from the subtropical high-pressure belts.

Monsoon

Seasonal reversal or change in wind direction associated with seasonal rainfall.

Equable climate

A climate in which temperature varies relatively little throughout the year.

Extreme climate

A climate with a comparatively large difference between temperatures in different seasons.

Temperature range

The difference between the highest and lowest temperatures over a stated period.

Convectional rainfall

Rainfall caused when heated air rises, cools and condenses. The supplied solutions HTML gives a general explanation, but the textbook’s detailed discussion focuses mainly on monsoon and orographic rainfall.

How to read a climate graph

Read the month labels along the horizontal axis. Identify the highest and lowest temperature points on the temperature curves and subtract to calculate temperature range. For rainfall, compare the height of the monthly bars and identify the wettest month or season. Do not confuse the temperature scale with the rainfall scale.

What type of rainfall occurs because of this obstruction?

Orographic rainfall on the windward coastal side.

Correlate winds and rainfall.

Moist trade winds bring rainfall to coastal and windward regions. After crossing the highlands, the winds have less moisture, so rainfall is low in the rain-shadow region called the Drought Quadrilateral.

Towards the southern part of Brazil, near the Tropic of Capricorn, according to the textbook.

In which direction does temperature increase?

Broadly from the northern and high-altitude regions towards the southern and lower-altitude regions, although local conditions vary.

Which winds are responsible for rainfall in India?

The monsoon winds, especially the moisture-laden South-West Monsoon; the North-East or retreating monsoon also brings rainfall to parts of the Peninsula.

Why is part of Rajasthan desert?

The monsoon winds blow roughly parallel to the Aravalis and do not give much rain to parts of Gujarat and Rajasthan. These areas also lie away from the strongest moisture-bearing flow.

10. Textbook Exercise — Q2: State Right or Wrong and Rewrite

(a) Brazil lying on the equator affects its climate in a big way.

Right. The equator crosses northern Brazil, contributing to high temperatures in the north.

(b) India and Brazil have the same seasons at the same time.

Wrong. India and Brazil are in different hemispheric settings and their seasonal patterns do not occur at the same time in the same way.

(c) India faces tropical cyclones frequently.

Right according to the supplied solutions HTML. The textbook notes that India faces cyclones among its natural disasters.

(d) Brazil gets a lot of rainfall because of the south-west monsoon winds.

Wrong. Brazil receives rainfall from the South-East Trade Winds and North-East Trade Winds.

Right. Manaus is near the equator and its temperature curve shows relatively small annual variation.

(f) India receives precipitation from the North-East Monsoon winds too.

Right. During the retreating monsoon, north-easterly winds bring rainfall to parts of the Peninsula, particularly Tamil Nadu.

11. Textbook Exercise — Q3: Give Geographical Reasons

(a) The north-eastern part of the Brazilian Highlands receives very little rainfall.

The Brazilian Highlands obstruct moisture-bearing winds coming from the sea. The windward coastal side receives orographic rainfall; beyond the highlands, the winds have less moisture. The northeastern highlands fall in the rain-shadow area called the Drought Quadrilateral.

(b) Snowfall does not always occur in Brazil.

Brazil is mainly tropical, so its temperatures are usually too high for snowfall. Snowfall occurs only exceptionally when southern polar air masses reach the southern part of the country.

(c) Convectional rainfall is not prominent in India.

The supplied solutions HTML says India’s rainfall is primarily driven by the monsoon mechanism, including orographic and cyclonic processes. This answer follows the supplied solution; the textbook chapter does not develop this comparison in detail.

(d) Tropical cyclones occur rarely in Brazil.

The textbook states that the convergence zone of trade winds is weak near the equator and that winds can move vertically there. As a result, cyclones rarely form or visit Brazil’s coast.

(e) There is not much difference in the range of temperature in Manaus.

Manaus is near the equator. The graph shows high temperatures through the year with relatively small seasonal variation.

(f) India receives precipitation from North-East Monsoon winds too.

During the retreating monsoon, winds blow from the north-east towards the Indian Ocean and bring rainfall to parts of the Peninsula. The textbook notes that Tamil Nadu receives maximum rainfall during this season.

12. Textbook Exercise — Q4: Answer the Following

(a) Describe climatic changes from south to north in India.

Temperatures generally decrease as one moves north, and they decrease further with increasing altitude in mountainous regions. The south is generally warmer, while northern and Himalayan regions experience colder winters. The textbook notes very low winter temperatures in Jammu and Kashmir and the Himalayas.

(b) Explain the importance of the Himalayas and Indian Ocean for India’s climate.

The Himalayas obstruct very cold winds from the north and influence the movement of monsoon winds. The Indian Ocean is the source region for moisture-laden monsoon winds that bring rainfall to India. Mountain barriers such as the Western and Eastern Ghats cause orographic rainfall, while their leeward sides receive less rainfall.

(c) Discuss the factors affecting Brazil’s climate.

The textbook highlights Brazil’s latitudinal extent, the equator crossing the north, the Tropic of Capricorn near the south, the South-East and North-East Trade Winds, the Atlantic coastal influence, and the Brazilian Highlands and Great Escarpment. These factors create hot northern conditions, cooler highlands and southern areas, humid coastal climates, orographic rainfall and a dry rain-shadow region.

(d) Compare the climates of Brazil and India.

India has a monsoon-type climate, strongly influenced by seasonal monsoon winds, the Himalayas and the Indian Ocean. Brazil’s climate is influenced by its latitudinal extent, trade winds, Atlantic coast and Brazilian Highlands. Both countries have regional differences in temperature and rainfall. Brazil’s Amazon Valley is hot and wet, while India’s rainfall is strongly seasonal and varies greatly between windward, leeward, coastal and inland regions.
