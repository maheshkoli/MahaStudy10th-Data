1. Chapter at a Glance

A lens is a transparent medium bounded by two surfaces. Light is refracted twice while passing through a lens: once when entering and once when emerging. The two main types are convex and concave lenses. A lens can converge or diverge light and form images whose position, size and nature depend on the lens and the object's position.

Convex lens

Thicker at the centre; converges parallel rays.

Concave lens

Thinner at the centre; diverges parallel rays.

Lens formula

Connects object distance, image distance and focal length.

Human eye

Uses a crystalline lens to focus light on the retina.

2. Types of Lenses and Important Terms

Types of lenses

  • Convex (double-convex) lens: Both surfaces bulge outward. It is thicker at the centre than at the edges and is a converging lens.
  • Concave (double-concave) lens: Both surfaces curve inward. It is thinner at the centre than at the edges and is a diverging lens.
  • The textbook also shows biconvex, plano-convex, positive meniscus, biconcave, plano-concave and negative meniscus shapes (Figure 7.2, page 80).

Definitions

TermMeaning
Centre of curvature (C)The centres of the spheres whose parts form the lens surfaces. A lens with two spherical surfaces has C₁ and C₂.
Radius of curvature (R)The radii R₁ and R₂ of the spheres that form the lens surfaces.
Principal axisThe imaginary line passing through both centres of curvature.
Optical centre (O)The point on the principal axis through which a ray passes without changing its direction.
Principal focus (F)For a convex lens, parallel rays converge at the focus. For a concave lens, refracted rays diverge and appear to originate from the focus.
Focal length (f)The distance between the optical centre and the principal focus.
Remember: A convex lens is called a converging lens; a concave lens is called a diverging lens.

3. Ray Diagrams and Image Formation

Three principal rays for a convex lens

  1. A ray parallel to the principal axis refracts through the principal focus on the other side.
  2. A ray passing through the principal focus before reaching the lens emerges parallel to the principal axis.
  3. A ray passing through the optical centre continues without changing direction.

These three rules are used to locate an image in a ray diagram. The textbook illustrates them in Figure 7.5 and demonstrates a convex-lens experiment with a candle and screen in Figure 7.6 (pages 81–82).

Image formed by a concave lens

A ray parallel to the principal axis diverges after refraction and appears to come from the focus on the same side as the object. A ray through the optical centre continues in the same direction. The image formed by a concave lens is always virtual, erect and smaller than the object. For an object at infinity, the image is at the first focus; for an object at any finite distance, it lies between the optical centre and focus (Figure 7.9, page 84).

4. Sign Convention, Lens Formula and Magnification

Cartesian sign convention

  • The optical centre O is the origin, and the principal axis is the X-axis.
  • The object is placed to the left of the lens; distances are measured from O.
  • Distances measured to the right of O are positive; distances to the left are negative.
  • Heights above the principal axis are positive; heights below it are negative.
  • Focal length is positive for a convex lens and negative for a concave lens.

Lens formula

1/v − 1/u = 1/f

u = object distance, v = image distance, and f = focal length. Use the sign convention consistently when substituting values.

Magnification

M = image height / object height = h₂ / h₁ = v / u

A negative magnification indicates an inverted image under the textbook's sign convention. A positive magnification indicates an erect image.

5. Power and Combination of Lenses

The power of a lens is its ability to converge or diverge incident rays. It is the reciprocal of the focal length measured in metres.

P = 1 / f (metres)    Unit: dioptre (D)
  • Convex lens: positive focal length and positive power.
  • Concave lens: negative focal length and negative power.

Two lenses in contact

1/f = 1/f₁ + 1/f₂
P = P₁ + P₂

For several thin lenses in contact, add their powers algebraically, including their signs.

6. Human Eye and Its Working

  • Cornea: Transparent front covering; the textbook says most refraction entering the eye occurs at its outer surface.
  • Iris: Coloured, muscular structure behind the cornea.
  • Pupil: Opening in the iris. It becomes smaller in bright light and wider in dim light, controlling the amount of light entering.
  • Crystalline lens: Transparent, double-convex lens that makes small focal-length adjustments to focus the image.
  • Ciliary muscles: Change the curvature of the eye lens.
  • Retina: Light-sensitive screen where a real, inverted image forms.
  • Optic nerve: Carries signals from the retina to the brain.

The textbook’s eye diagram (Figure 7.11, page 87) labels the cornea, iris, pupil, crystalline lens, ciliary muscles, retina and optic nerve.

Power of accommodation

The capacity of the eye lens to change its focal length is called the power of accommodation. When looking at distant objects, the lens becomes flatter and its focal length increases. When looking at nearby objects, it becomes rounder and its focal length decreases.

Near point

For a normal eye, 25 cm—the closest distance at which an object can be seen clearly without strain.

Far point

For a normal eye, infinity—the farthest distance at which an object can be seen clearly without strain.

In bifocal spectacles described in the textbook, the upper part is concave to correct nearsightedness and the lower part is convex to correct farsightedness (page 89).

8. Uses of Lenses and Optical Instruments

Uses of concave lenses

  • Door peepholes to provide a wider view outside.
  • Spectacles to correct nearsightedness.
  • Torches to spread light from a small bulb.
  • The textbook also mentions uses in some medical equipment, scanners, CD players, cameras, telescopes and microscopes.

Uses of convex lenses

  • Simple microscope: A short-focal-length convex lens produces an enlarged, erect, virtual image. Used in watch repair and examining gems.
  • Compound microscope: Uses an objective and an eyepiece, both convex lenses. The first image becomes the object for the second lens, giving greater magnification.
  • Telescope: Used to view distant objects in magnified form. A refracting telescope uses lenses; a reflecting telescope uses mirrors as well as lenses. The objective collects light and forms an image that serves as the eyepiece's object.
  • Other instruments: Convex lenses are used in cameras, projectors and spectrographs, and in spectacles for farsightedness.

Persistence of vision and colour

Persistence of vision: The textbook states that the image remains on the retina for about 1/16 of a second after the object is removed. This helps explain visual effects such as a rapidly rotated glowing object appearing as a continuous circle.

Colour vision: Rod-like cells respond to light intensity, while cone-like cells respond to colour. The textbook explains that cones respond differently to red, green and blue light, and that colour is perceived most effectively in bright light.

9. Worked Examples

Example 1 — Image formed by a convex lens

Given: u = −20 cm, h₁ = 5 cm, f = +10 cm.

Using the lens formula:

1/v − 1/u = 1/f
1/v − 1/(−20) = 1/10 ⇒ 1/v + 1/20 = 1/10
1/v = 1/10 − 1/20 = 1/20 ⇒ v = +20 cm

Magnification: M = v/u = 20/(−20) = −1.

h₂ = M × h₁ = (−1) × 5 = −5 cm

Answer: Image is 20 cm on the other side, 5 cm high, real, inverted and the same size as the object.

Example 2 — Power of a convex lens

Given: f = +20 cm = +0.20 m.

P = 1/f = 1/0.20 = +5 D

Answer: +5 dioptres.

Q2. Draw a figure explaining various terms related to a lens.

Answer guidance: Draw a horizontal principal axis and a lens at the centre. Mark the optical centre O, the principal foci F₁ and F₂ on either side, and centres of curvature C₁ and C₂. Label the distances from O to the foci as focal length f. For a lens with spherical surfaces, also label radii R₁ and R₂.

Q3. Where should an object be placed before a convex lens to get a real image of the same size? Draw a figure.

Answer: Place the object at 2F₁. The image forms at 2F₂ on the other side of the lens. It is real, inverted and the same size as the object (see the image-formation table on textbook page 83).

Q4(a). Why is a simple microscope used for watch repairs?

A simple microscope uses a convex lens of short focal length to form an enlarged, erect, virtual image of a small object. This makes tiny watch components easier to see.

Q4(b). Why can we sense colours only in bright light?

According to the textbook, rod cells respond to faint light and mainly provide information about brightness, while cone cells respond to colours. Cone cells work effectively in bright light, so colour vision is poor in dim light.

Q4(c). Why can we not clearly see an object kept closer than 25 cm to the eye?

For a normal eye, 25 cm is the near point—the minimum distance for clear vision without strain. When an object is brought closer, the eye lens may not be able to increase its converging power enough to focus the image clearly on the retina.

Q5. Explain the working of an astronomical telescope using refraction of light.

An astronomical refracting telescope uses two convex lenses: an objective lens and an eyepiece. The objective has a large diameter and long focal length so it can collect light from a distant object and form a real image. This image acts as the object for the eyepiece, which magnifies it for viewing. The two lenses are aligned along the same principal axis inside a tube, and their separation can be adjusted for focusing.

Q7. What is the function of the iris and the muscles connected to the lens?

The iris controls the size of the pupil and therefore the amount of light entering the eye. The ciliary muscles change the curvature of the crystalline lens, adjusting its focal length so near and distant objects can be focused on the retina.

Q8(i). A lens has power +1.5 D. Find its focal length, type and the vision defect it corrects.

f = 1/P = 1/1.5 = +0.666… m ≈ +0.67 m

Answer: Focal length ≈ +0.67 m; convex (converging) lens; used to correct farsightedness (hypermetropia), as given in the textbook answer.

Q8(ii). A 5 cm high object is 25 cm from a converging lens of focal length 10 cm. Find image position, size and type.

Given u = −25 cm, f = +10 cm, h₁ = 5 cm.

1/v − 1/(−25) = 1/10 ⇒ 1/v + 1/25 = 1/10
1/v = 1/10 − 1/25 = 3/50 ⇒ v = +50/3 ≈ +16.7 cm
M = v/u = 16.7/(−25) ≈ −0.667
h₂ = M × h₁ ≈ −0.667 × 5 ≈ −3.3 cm

Answer: Image position ≈ 16.7 cm on the opposite side; image height ≈ 3.3 cm (negative sign means inverted); image is real and inverted.

Q8(iii). Three lenses of powers 2 D, 2.5 D and 1.7 D are in contact. Find total power.

P = P₁ + P₂ + P₃ = 2 + 2.5 + 1.7 = +6.2 D

Answer: +6.2 D.

Q8(iv). An object 60 cm from a lens forms a virtual image 20 cm in front of the lens. Find focal length and lens type.

Using the textbook's sign convention, u = −60 cm and v = −20 cm.

1/f = 1/v − 1/u = 1/(−20) − 1/(−60)
1/f = −1/20 + 1/60 = −3/60 + 1/60 = −1/30
f = −30 cm

Answer: Focal length = −30 cm; it is a diverging (concave) lens.

11. Quick Revision Sheet

  • Convex lens: converging; focal length and power are positive.
  • Concave lens: diverging; focal length and power are negative.
  • Lens formula: 1/v − 1/u = 1/f.
  • Magnification: M = h₂/h₁ = v/u.
  • Power: P = 1/f, with f in metres; unit is dioptre (D).
  • Lenses in contact: combined power P = P₁ + P₂ (+ any further lens powers).
  • Normal eye near point: 25 cm; far point: infinity.
  • Myopia: corrected by a concave lens.
  • Hypermetropia: corrected by a convex lens.
  • Presbyopia: age-related loss of accommodation; bifocal lenses may be needed for near and distance correction.
  • Simple microscope: convex lens; enlarged, erect, virtual image.
  • Persistence of vision: about 1/16 second, according to the textbook.
Numerical tip: Convert focal length to metres before calculating power. Apply signs to u, v and f before using the lens formula.
