1. Chapter at a Glance

This chapter features the famous monologue "All the World's a Stage" from William Shakespeare's play As You Like It. The poem uses the metaphor of a stage to describe the human life cycle, dividing it into seven distinct stages. Students will learn to analyze poetic devices like metaphor and imagery, understand the structure of a dramatic monologue, and reflect on the different phases of human existence.

2. Important Concepts Explained

  • The Metaphor of the Stage: The poet compares the world to a stage and all human beings to actors. Just as actors have entrances (birth) and exits (death), humans play various roles throughout their lives.
  • The Seven Ages of Man: Shakespeare divides life into seven distinct stages, each with its own characteristics, behaviors, and roles:
  1. The Infant: Helpless, crying, and vomiting in the nurse's arms.
  2. The Schoolboy: Unwilling to go to school, creeping slowly like a snail, carrying his satchel.
  3. The Lover: Emotional, sighing like a furnace, writing sad poems for his beloved.
  4. The Soldier: Quick-tempered, bearded, seeking glory and reputation, even at the risk of death ("the cannon's mouth").
  5. The Justice: Mature, serious, well-fed (round belly), full of wise sayings and modern examples.
  6. The Pantaloon: Old, thin, wearing spectacles and a pouch, his clothes becoming too big for his shrunken frame.
  7. Second Childishness: Extreme old age, losing teeth, sight, and taste, leading to oblivion (forgetfulness) and the end of the "eventful history."

3. Definitions and Key Terms

  • Metaphor: A figure of speech where a word or phrase is applied to an object or action to which it is not literally applicable (e.g., "All the world's a stage").
  • Blank Verse: Poetry written in regular metrical but unrhymed lines, almost always in iambic pentameter.
  • Mewling: Crying feebly.
  • Satchel: A bag carried on the shoulder.
  • Pard: A leopard (often used in poetry).
  • Capon: A castrated rooster, often used as a symbol of luxury or over-eating.
  • Saws: Sayings or proverbs.
  • Pantaloon: A stock character in comedy, representing a foolish old man.
  • Treble: A high-pitched voice.
  • Oblivion: The state of being unaware or unconscious of surroundings.

4. Formulas, Rules, and Properties

  • Structure: The poem is written in Blank Verse, meaning it does not have a rhyme scheme but maintains a steady rhythm (iambic pentameter, with 5 beats per line).

7. Questions from the Poem

What do “exits” and “entrances” refer to?
They refer to death and birth, respectively. People enter the world at birth and exit it at death.

Why is reputation like a bubble?
A bubble is fragile and can burst easily. Similarly, reputation may be temporary and can disappear quickly, even after a person risks their life to gain it.

What is the major difference between the fifth and sixth stages of life?
In the fifth stage, the man is a mature “justice,” well-fed, serious and full of wise sayings. In the sixth stage, he becomes old, thin and weak, wears spectacles and loose clothes, and his voice becomes higher and weaker.

3. Differences between the stages

Second stage and fourth stage: The schoolboy is young, innocent and reluctant to attend school. The soldier is mature, ambitious, brave and quick-tempered, and seeks honour and reputation even in danger.

Third stage and fifth stage: The lover is ruled by romantic feelings and sighs dramatically for his beloved. The justice is mature, serious, well-fed and respected, and often gives wise sayings and examples.

First stage and seventh stage: The infant is at the beginning of life, helpless but expected to grow. In the final stage, the elderly person returns to a state of helplessness and loses many physical senses and abilities.

5. Blank verse and stress pattern

The poem has no regular rhyme scheme but follows a steady rhythm of approximately five beats per line, known as blank verse (traditionally iambic pentameter). Stress may vary naturally in performance. A sample scansion of the opening line is:

And ALL the MEN and WOM-en MERE-ly PLAY-ers

For classroom work, mark the stressed syllables while reading the specified lines aloud; different editions and spoken emphasis may vary slightly.

6. Think and write

(a) Theme / central idea: Shakespeare compares the world to a stage and human beings to actors. Each person enters life, performs different roles as they grow older, and eventually leaves the world. The poem reflects the changes that occur throughout the seven stages of human life.

(b) Which two stages sound humorous, and why?
The schoolboy and the justice can sound humorous. The schoolboy’s slow, unwilling movement to school is exaggerated, while the justice’s round belly and serious appearance are described in a vivid, slightly comic way. The lover’s exaggerated sighs can also be read humorously.

(c) How can old age be made cheerful and happy?
Old age can be made happier through family support, friendship, respect, regular suitable activity, hobbies, social involvement and access to healthcare. Listening to older people and valuing their experience also helps them feel included.

8. “As You Like It”: speaker and characters

The speech “All the world’s a stage” is spoken by Jaques in William Shakespeare’s play As You Like It, in Act II, Scene VII. He delivers it during a reflective conversation about human life and its stages.

Main characters in the play: Rosalind, Orlando, Celia, Oliver, Jaques, Touchstone, Duke Senior, Duke Frederick, Amiens, Le Beau, Charles the wrestler, Adam, Dennis, Silvius, Phoebe, Corin, William, Audrey, Hymen and the First and Second Lords, along with other attendants and minor characters. Editions may vary in how they list minor roles.

9. Appreciation of the poem

The poem “All the World’s a Stage” is written by William Shakespeare and is taken from his play As You Like It. The poem compares the world to a stage and human beings to actors who play different roles throughout their lives. Shakespeare divides life into seven stages: infant, schoolboy, lover, soldier, justice, pantaloon and second childishness. The poem uses vivid imagery, similes and metaphors to describe the physical and emotional changes that come with age. The line “creeping like snail” shows the schoolboy’s reluctance to go to school, while “bubble reputation” suggests the fragile nature of fame. The poem is written in blank verse, with no regular rhyme scheme but a steady rhythm. Its central idea is that life is temporary and people change as they move from one stage to another. The poem encourages readers to reflect on the passage of time and the different roles people perform in society.

9. Important Questions and Answers

Why does Shakespeare compare the world to a stage?
Because people, like actors, enter the world, perform different roles through life and eventually leave it.

What does “bubble reputation” mean?
It means fame or reputation is fragile and can disappear quickly, like a bubble that bursts.

What is blank verse?
Blank verse is poetry without a regular rhyme scheme but with a regular metrical rhythm, traditionally iambic pentameter.

What is meant by “second childishness”?
It is the final stage of life, when a very old person may lose physical abilities and become dependent, resembling a child in helplessness.
