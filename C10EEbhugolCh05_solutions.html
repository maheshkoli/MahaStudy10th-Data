1. Chapter at a Glance

Main idea

This chapter studies the relationship between climate, physiography, natural vegetation and wildlife in India and Brazil. It explains how rainfall and altitude influence forest types and animal habitats, and discusses threats to forests and wildlife.

Brazil

Rainfall is high through much of the equatorial region and decreases farther from the Equator. Brazil has evergreen, semi-evergreen, arid and other vegetation types. The chapter highlights Pau Brasil, rubber, mahogany, rosewood and orchids, and describes Brazil's wildlife as highly diverse.

India

India has evergreen, deciduous, thorny and shrub-type, coastal and Himalayan vegetation. The type of vegetation depends on rainfall, dry seasons, salinity near coasts and altitude in the Himalayas.

Conservation

The chapter identifies deforestation, pollution, poaching, illegal wildlife smuggling and slash-and-burn agriculture as threats. It describes national parks, wildlife sanctuaries, bird sanctuaries and biosphere reserves as protection measures in India.

2. Brazil — Natural Vegetation

Rainfall and vegetation

In most parts of the equatorial region, rain occurs throughout the year. As distance from the Equator increases, the number of rainy days and the amount of rainfall reduce. This affects vegetation and its life cycle.

Evergreen rainforests

Evergreen forests occur where it rains throughout the year. The chapter lists Pau Brasil, rubber, mahogany, rosewood and many orchids. It describes these forests as having high biodiversity and calls Brazil's evergreen rainforests the 'lungs of the world' because they release oxygen and help reduce carbon dioxide levels.

Other vegetation

The chapter says Brazil has a large number of vegetation species, including evergreen, semi-evergreen and arid vegetation. Where rainfall is seasonal or lower, vegetation becomes less dense and may include grasses, short shrubs and thorny plants.

North-to-south change (Figure 5.2, textbook page 32)

The cross-profile shows equatorial forests around the Amazon Basin, with grassland animals and other vegetation farther south, and deciduous/temperate forest areas towards the highlands and southern latitudes. It also shows changes in rainfall and temperature along the profile.

3. India — Natural Vegetation

Evergreen forests

Found in regions receiving more than 2,000 mm of rainfall on average along with abundant sunlight. Trees have broad green leaves and hardwood. Examples given in the chapter include mahogany, rosewood and rubber.

Deciduous forests

Found in regions receiving about 1,000–2,000 mm of rainfall. Trees shed their leaves during dry seasons to reduce water loss through evaporation. Examples include teak, bamboo, banyan and peepal.

Thorny and shrub-type vegetation

Found in regions receiving less than 500 mm of rainfall and experiencing long dry summers. Plants often have small leaves. Examples include catechu, acacia, khejri and cactus varieties such as aloe vera and agave.

Coastal vegetation

Found in swampy areas, estuaries and lagoons near coasts where soils are saline and the climate is moist. The chapter refers to this vegetation as mangroves or the Sunderbans in India.

Himalayan forests

Vegetation changes with altitude. At the highest altitudes, seasonally flowering plants are found. At medium altitudes, conifers such as pine, deodar and fir occur. At the foothills, mixed forests include both coniferous and deciduous trees; the chapter notes a higher proportion of sal trees.

4. Brazil — Wildlife

High diversity

The textbook describes Brazil as having very rich and diverse wildlife, including mammals, reptiles, birds, fish and insects.

Animals named in the chapter

Anacondas are associated with swampy areas of the Pantanal. The text also names guinea pigs, crocodiles, alligators, monkeys, pumas and leopards. Swordfish are mentioned in the seas, while pink dolphins and piranhas are found in rivers. Birds include condors, parrots, macaws and flamingoes.

Habitats

The map in Figure 5.4 (textbook page 34) shows equatorial forests, tropical grasslands, swampy lands, hot deciduous forests, thorny shrubs and temperate grasslands. These different environments support different wildlife.

Threats

The textbook lists illegal smuggling of wild animals, slash-and-burn agriculture (Roca), deforestation and pollution. It states that many endemic species are at risk of extinction.

5. India — Wildlife

Examples and habitats

Elephants are found in hot, humid forests. One-horned rhinoceroses are associated with swampy and marshy lands of Assam. Wild asses and camels occur in arid lands. Snow leopards and yaks are found in snow-capped Himalayan regions. Indian bisons, deer, antelopes and monkeys are found in the Peninsular region.

Birds and aquatic animals

The chapter mentions peacocks, Indian bustards, kingfishers, pheasants, ducks, parakeets, cranes and pigeons. Rivers, estuaries and coastal areas provide habitats for turtles, crocodiles and gharials.

Threats and protection

The chapter says wildlife is threatened by poaching, pollution and rapid deforestation, and mentions cheetahs as an example. India has established national parks, wildlife sanctuaries, bird sanctuaries and biosphere reserves to protect forests and wildlife.

Tiger and lion

The textbook states that India is the only country in the world where both tigers and lions are found.

6. Important Terms

Natural vegetation

Plant life that grows naturally in an area, influenced by climate, rainfall, soil and relief.

Flora and fauna

Flora means plant life; fauna means animal life.

Biodiversity

The variety of plant and animal life in a region or ecosystem.

Evergreen forest

A forest type found in the chapter's description in areas with rainfall throughout the year; trees do not all shed their leaves in one dry season.

Deciduous forest

A forest type in which trees shed leaves during a dry season to reduce water loss.

Thorny and shrub-type vegetation

Vegetation adapted to low rainfall and long dry periods, often with small leaves and thorny plants.

Mangroves

Coastal vegetation associated with swampy, saline environments such as estuaries and lagoons.

Coniferous trees

Cone-bearing trees; the chapter gives pine, deodar and fir as examples in the Himalayas.

Endemic species

A species native to a particular geographic region. The chapter warns that some such species are threatened.

Roca

The name used in Brazil for shifting agriculture in which forest land is cleared by cutting and burning, and subsistence farming is carried out on the patch for a few years.

11. Textbook Exercise — Q4: Answer in Short

(a) Differentiate between the forest types of Brazil and India.

Brazil has extensive equatorial forests around the Amazon Basin, along with tropical grasslands, swampy lands, hot deciduous forests, thorny shrubs and temperate grasslands. India has evergreen, deciduous, thorny and shrub-type, coastal and Himalayan forests. In both countries, vegetation changes with rainfall and physical conditions; in India, altitude is especially important in the Himalayas.

(b) Correlate wildlife and natural vegetation in India and Brazil.

Wildlife depends on habitat. Brazil's equatorial forests support many forest animals and birds, while its Pantanal wetlands are associated with anacondas and other wetland wildlife. In India, hot and humid forests support elephants, Assam's marshy lands support one-horned rhinoceroses, arid regions support camels and wild asses, and Himalayan snow regions support snow leopards and yaks. Rivers and coasts support turtles, crocodiles and gharials.

(c) What environmental issues are faced by Brazil and India?

The chapter mentions deforestation, pollution, illegal wildlife smuggling or poaching, habitat loss and threats to endemic species. Brazil also faces forest degradation associated with slash-and-burn agriculture, called Roca.

(d) What are the major causes of degradation of forests in Brazil and India?

The chapter identifies deforestation, pollution, illegal wildlife smuggling and slash-and-burn agriculture (Roca in Brazil) as environmental problems. These activities damage habitats and threaten plant and animal species. The chapter does not provide a complete ranked list of causes for both countries.

(e) Why does deciduous vegetation occupy most of India?

The supplied solutions HTML explains that much of India receives about 1,000–2,000 mm of rainfall, a range associated in the chapter with deciduous forests. These trees shed their leaves during dry seasons to reduce water loss.

12. Textbook Exercise — Q5: Give Geographical Reasons

(a) The northern part of Brazil is covered by dense forests.

The textbook says most of the equatorial region receives rain throughout the year. This high, regular rainfall supports dense evergreen forests in the Amazon region.

(b) Vegetation is scarce at high altitudes in the Himalayas.

The chapter notes that vegetation changes with altitude and that only certain types grow at the highest levels. Very high mountain regions have severe cold and seasonal snow, which limit plant growth; this last explanation is a geographical inference consistent with the chapter's altitude-based description.

(c) A wide variety of insect species is found in Brazil.

The textbook says Brazil has very rich and diverse wildlife and millions of insect varieties. Its varied vegetation and habitats provide places for many species to live; the chapter does not give a detailed insect-specific explanation.

(d) Wildlife in India is decreasing day by day.

The chapter links wildlife decline to poaching, pollution and rapidly occurring deforestation, which damage habitats and threaten species.

(e) Like India, there is a need to conserve forests in Brazil too.

The chapter describes high biodiversity in Brazil and says forest degradation threatens endemic species. Conserving forests protects habitats and the plants and animals that depend on them.

13. Quick Revision Sheet

India's forest types

Evergreen (>2,000 mm rainfall with abundant sunlight); deciduous (about 1,000–2,000 mm); thorny/shrub-type (<500 mm and long dry summers); coastal mangroves; Himalayan vegetation changing with altitude.

Brazil's vegetation

Evergreen equatorial forests, semi-evergreen and arid vegetation, grasslands and other vegetation zones that change with rainfall and physiography.

Wildlife examples

Brazil: anaconda, piranha, pink dolphin, macaw and condor. India: elephant, one-horned rhinoceros, camel, wild ass, snow leopard, yak, tiger and lion.

Threats

Deforestation, pollution, poaching, illegal wildlife smuggling and Roca/slash-and-burn agriculture.

Conservation in India

National parks, wildlife sanctuaries, bird sanctuaries and biosphere reserves.

Key term

Roca = Brazilian shifting agriculture in which forest land is cut and burned before subsistence farming.
