1. Poem at a Glance

Poet: Nissim Ezekiel (1924–2004), an Indian Jewish poet, actor, playwright, editor and art critic.

The poem recalls the night the poet’s mother was stung by a scorpion during heavy rain. The villagers arrive and respond with traditional beliefs and prayers, while the father—described as a sceptic and rationalist—tries a range of remedies. After twenty hours, the mother finds relief. Her final words express selfless love: she thanks God that the scorpion stung her rather than her children.

The poem presents the villagers’ beliefs and the father’s actions through the speaker’s memory. Some interpretations of the villagers’ behaviour are inferential; the answers below stay close to the poem and the supplied English Workshop questions.

4. Evidence from the poem

StageEvidence / explanation
(a) Peasants’ attempts to alleviate the mother’s painThey “buzzed the name of God a hundred times”; they wished that her suffering would decrease the misfortunes of her next birth and that the poison would purify her flesh and spirit.
(b) Peasants’ action to kill the scorpion“they searched for him: he was not found.” The poem says they searched, but does not describe them killing it.
(c) Father’s reaction“trying every curse and blessing, / powder, mixture, herb and hybrid.” He also poured paraffin on the bitten toe and lit it.
(d) Superstitious versus “scientific” responsesThe peasants’ prayers and beliefs about previous and next births contrast with the father’s attempts using powders, mixtures, herbs and paraffin. The poem does not portray every remedy as strictly scientific.
(e) Evil versus goodThe scorpion’s “diabolic tail” and poison contrast with the mother’s selfless final words and her concern for her children.

6(B). Examples of figures of speech

  • Simile: “The peasants came like swarms of flies.” The word “like” compares the peasants to flies.
  • Metaphor: “May the sum of all evil / balanced in this unreal world / against the sum of good / become diminished by your pain.” This presents good and evil as quantities that can be balanced. The phrase “The poison purified your flesh” is part of the peasants’ belief, not a straightforward metaphor example.
  • Onomatopoeia: “buzzed” imitates a buzzing sound; “clicked” suggests the clicking sound of tongues.
  • Alliteration: “steady rain” repeats the initial /s/ sound; read aloud to hear the sound pattern.

7. Expand the flow chart into a paragraph

The mother experiences great agony after the scorpion stings her. The poison causes intense pain, and she twists and groans on a mat. Her family and the villagers try different remedies and rituals, while she endures the suffering. After twenty hours, the poison loses its sting and she survives. Her first words are a prayer of thanks that the scorpion chose her and spared her children. This final response reveals her selfless and unconditional maternal love.

4. Poem Analysis

Central themes

  • Maternal love: The mother’s final words show that her children’s safety matters more to her than her own suffering.
  • Superstition and rationalism: The villagers interpret the sting through religious and karmic beliefs; the father is called a rationalist but tries a mix of remedies under pressure.
  • Community response: The neighbours gather around the family, showing collective concern, although their explanations are superstitious.
  • Memory and perspective: The poem is narrated in the first person, recalling a frightening childhood event.

5. Appreciation of the Poem

“Night of the Scorpion” by Nissim Ezekiel

“Night of the Scorpion” is a narrative poem written in free verse. The poet uses first-person narration to recall the night when a scorpion stung his mother during heavy rain. The poem contrasts the peasants’ superstitious prayers with the father’s attempts to find a remedy, while also presenting the family’s fear and concern. Ezekiel uses vivid visual, tactile and sound imagery, as well as a simile in “The peasants came like swarms of flies.” Words such as “buzzed” and “clicked” contribute to sound imagery. The poem’s most moving moment is the mother’s final statement: “Thank God the scorpion picked on me / And spared my children.” It reveals her unconditional love and selflessness. The poem suggests that a mother’s love can remain strong even during intense pain.
