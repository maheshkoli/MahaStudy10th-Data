1. Chapter at a Glance

“Let’s March” is based on Kailash Satyarthi’s Nobel Peace Prize acceptance speech. He speaks about protecting children’s rights, ending child labour and other forms of violence, and ensuring education, dignity, freedom and safety for every child.

The speech asks people, governments, businesses, faith leaders, teachers and organisations to work together. Its key message is that every person can contribute, even through a small action. The story of a tiny bird trying to put out a forest fire illustrates the importance of doing one’s part.

The notes and answers below follow the supplied textbook chapter (printed pages 76–88) and the provided HTML. Personal-response questions include sample answers that students can adapt.

3. Warming Up

1. Observe the pictures and answer

(a) Main difference between the two images: One picture shows a child working at a brick site, while the other shows a child riding a bicycle. The first suggests child labour and lost childhood; the second suggests freedom and an opportunity to enjoy childhood.

(b) Change required: Children should be removed from labour and given access to education, safety, play and a healthy childhood.

(c) My contribution as a student: I can support education for every child, tell a trusted adult or appropriate authority if I see child labour, help with school supplies through responsible community efforts, and treat every child with respect.

5. Think and give your own response

(a) How can education help deprived children and child labourers?
Education helps children understand their rights, develop knowledge and skills, improve their future opportunities and escape the cycle of poverty. It also helps them grow into confident and responsible citizens.

(b) Name another social activist who works for child welfare.
Malala Yousafzai advocates for girls’ right to education. Through her public work, she encourages communities and governments to ensure that girls can attend school safely.

(c) What message does the little bird story convey?
The story teaches that even a small individual effort matters. Instead of waiting for someone else to act, we should do whatever we reasonably can to help solve a problem.

(d) Besides political freedom, what other freedom should our nation strive for?
India should strive for freedom from poverty, illiteracy, child labour, discrimination and exploitation so that everyone can live with dignity and equal opportunity.

(e) What is your impression of Satyarthi’s acceptance speech?
The speech is moving, direct and inspiring. It draws attention to children whose suffering is often ignored and urges people to act together. Its stories and repeated calls to march make its message memorable.

5. Grammar Solutions

9. What will you do in these situations?

(A)

  • (a) A child working in a restaurant: I would inform a trusted adult or the appropriate child-protection authority and help the child access support and education.
  • (b) A child working at a construction site: I would report the situation to the appropriate authority and avoid putting the child or myself at risk.
  • (c) A child working at a brickwork site: I would seek help from a trusted adult or child-welfare organisation so the child can be protected and enrolled in school.
  • (d) A beggar child: I would treat the child respectfully and connect them with a responsible local child-welfare service rather than making promises I cannot keep.

(B) Two efforts to enrol out-of-school children

  1. I will persuade parents to send their children to school.
  2. I will inform local teachers or community organisations about children who are not attending school.
  3. I will help share information about free education, school supplies and enrolment support.

12(A). Match active and passive voice sentences

Part A (passive)Part B (active)
1. He was kidnapped by an extremist militia.(c) An extremist militia kidnapped him.
2. The child was forced to kill his friends and family.(a) They forced the child to kill his friends and family.

12(B). Change into passive voice

  1. We can do it. → It can be done by us.
  2. Her angry question still shakes me. → I am still shaken by her angry question.
  3. Governments must make child-friendly policies. → Child-friendly policies must be made by governments.
  4. His answer made me angry. → I was made angry by his answer.

6. Sample Welcome Speech for Malala Yousafzai

Respected Principal, honourable chief guest Malala Yousafzai, teachers, parents and my dear friends, good morning to one and all.

It is a great honour to welcome Malala Yousafzai to our school’s Children’s Day celebration. My name is [Your Name], and I am a student of Class 10. Today, we have gathered to celebrate childhood and to remember that every child deserves safety, respect and education.

Malala has become a powerful voice for every girl’s right to learn. Her courage reminds us that education can change lives and that young people can stand up for what is right. Her journey inspires students to value learning and to help create a world where no child is denied a classroom because of poverty, discrimination or fear.

On behalf of our school, I warmly welcome you, Malala. We are grateful for your presence and hope your words will encourage us to work harder for children’s rights and education.

Thank you for listening. Once again, welcome to our school, and Happy Children’s Day to everyone.
