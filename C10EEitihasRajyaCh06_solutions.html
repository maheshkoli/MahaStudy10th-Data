6.1 Why Do We Need Entertainment?

Entertainment includes activities that refresh the mind and create happiness, such as hobbies, games, drama, films, writing and reading. Healthy, high-quality entertainment can support personality development, keep the mind fresh, increase energy and improve efficiency at work.

6.2 Folk Theatre

Puppetry (Kathputali)

Clay dolls have been found at archaeological sites of the Harappan civilisation and at sites in Egypt and Greece; the textbook suggests they may have been used as puppets. In ancient India, puppets were made from materials such as wood, wool, leather, horns and ivory.

  • Kathputali has traditions in Rajasthan and southern India.
  • The artists staging shows are found in Uttar Pradesh, Maharashtra, Rajasthan, Assam, West Bengal, Andhra Pradesh, Telangana, Karnataka and Kerala.
  • The sutradhara (narrator) has a crucial role in a successful show.
  • Shadow, hand, wooden and string puppets are used; light and sound effects support the performance.

Dashavatara theatre

Dashavatara is a folk theatre tradition of Maharashtra, performed in the Konkan and Goa after the harvest season. The stories are based on the ten incarnations of Vishnu: Matsya, Koorma, Varaha, Narasimha, Wamana, Parashurama, Ram, Krishna, Buddha and Kalki.

The sutradhara begins by invoking Lord Ganesha. The performance is mainly musical, follows traditional acting, make-up and costumes, and uses wooden masks for divine characters. It ends with breaking a dahihandi (an earthen pot of curds) and performing aarati.

Bhajan

Bhajan is devotional singing and chanting accompanied by instruments such as taal (cymbals), mridangam and pakhavaj. The textbook describes two types:

  • Chakri Bhajan: Devotees move in a circle and sing continuously.
  • Songi Bhajan: Singer-actors portray devotees and deliver dialogues as devotional songs.

Tukadoji Maharaj introduced Khanjiri Bhajan. The chapter also names traditions of devotional compositions associated with Tulsidas, Surdas, Meerabai, Kabir and other saints, and describes Saint Namdev’s contribution to bhajan-keertan in Maharashtra.

Keertan

The textbook identifies Naradmuni as the traditional founder of Keertan and Saint Namdev as the first keertankar of Maharashtra. A keertankar, also known as Haridas or Kathekaribuva, combines oratory, singing, musical instruments, dance and humour.

  • Naraadiya / Haridasi Keertan: A solo performance with Poorvarang and Uttarrang.
  • Varkari Keertan: Gives greater importance to collective participation, including the cymbal players.
  • Rashtriya Keertan: Developed during the independence movement to create awareness through stories of leaders, scientists and social reformers; the tradition was started by Dattopant Patwardhan of Wai.

The chapter notes that members of the Satyashodhak Samaj used Keertan to create social awareness and that Saint Gadge Maharaj addressed issues such as caste abolition, cleanliness and de-addiction.

Lalit

Lalit is an old form of entertainment in Maharashtra linked to Naraadiya Keertan and popular in Goa and Konkan. It is performed theatrically during religious festivals and may present stories of Krishna, Rama and devotees. The textbook describes its traditional invocation as a wish for community happiness, clear minds and the end of internal conflict.

Bharud

Bharud is a metaphorical song carrying spiritual and ethical lessons. The textbook compares its style to a road show and highlights Saint Eknath’s compositions for their varied subjects, dramatic quality, easy rhythm and humour.

Tamasha

Tamasha is described as a Persian word meaning “a pleasing sight.” It developed as an independent art form in the 18th century by absorbing folk theatre and classical-art elements.

  • Sangeet Bari: Dance and music are more important than drama.
  • Dholakicha Phad: A traditional Tamasha form; a dramatic section called Vag later became an important feature.

A Tamasha show begins with Gana, praise of Lord Ganesha, followed by Gavalan and then Vag. The textbook gives Vichchha Mazi Puri Kara and Gadhavache Lagna as examples of the modern form.

Powada

Powada is a dramatic narration that alternates poetry and prose to describe the heroic deeds of men and women in an inspiring style. The chapter mentions Adnyandas’s Powada about the killing of Afzal Khan and Tulasidas’s Powada about the battle of Sinhagad. During the British period and the Samyukta Maharashtra movement, Powadas were also used to create public awareness.

6.3 Marathi Theatre

Theatre is a place devoted to solo or collective performance of the performing arts. A successful production requires artists and audience, as well as elements such as script, director, make-up, costumes, stage, art design, lighting and critics. Drama usually uses dialogue, though mime can also express meaning.

Origins and development

  • The textbook traces the origins of Marathi theatre to the Dashavatara tradition.
  • In the 18th century, Shyamji Naik Kale organised a phad (troupe) of Dashavatara artists who performed across Maharashtra.
  • Vishnudas Bhave, described as the pioneer or father of Marathi theatre, staged mythological plays with modifications to Dashavatara. His first play was Seetaswayamvar.
  • Initially, full written scripts were not used and dialogue was often spontaneous.
  • V. J. Kirtane wrote the script for Thorale Madhavrao Peshwe in 1861; its printed copy marked the beginning of the tradition of preparing a complete script before staging a play.

Musical Marathi theatre and important plays

In the latter half of the 19th century, Balkrishnbuva Ichalkaranjikar and later Ustad Alladiya Khan, Ustad Abdul Karim Khan and Ustad Rahimatkhan helped popularise classical khyal music in Maharashtra. The growth of interest in classical music contributed to musical Marathi theatre.

Closed theatres

Early Marathi plays were staged in open grounds. The textbook says the British first built closed theatres in Mumbai, including Play House, Rippon and Victoria, after which Marathi plays gradually moved to enclosed theatres.

6.4 Indian Film Industry

Cinema combines art and technology. Motion-picture technology led to silent movies, followed by sound movies when sound recording was introduced.

Early Indian cinema

  • Pundalik (1912): The textbook says Dadasaheb Torane and collaborators made the film with help from foreign technicians and released it in Mumbai.
  • Raja Harischandra (1913): Directed by Dadasaheb Phalke and released in Mumbai. The textbook identifies it as the first film to be processed completely in India.
  • Dadasaheb Phalke: Made silent films including Mohini-Bhasmasur and Savitri-Satyavana, as well as documentaries on rock-cut caves and pilgrimage centres.
  • Baburao Painter: Also known as “Mistri,” he made Sairandhri in 1918 and the historical film Simhgarh, as stated in the chapter.
  • Bajirao-Mastani (1925): Made by Bhalaji Pendharkar; the textbook says it was banned by the British government because it was suspected of spreading nationalist sentiments.
  • Women in production: Kamalabai Mangarulkar is identified in the chapter as the first woman producer.

Historical and documentary films

The textbook names Ramshastri (1944), Sant Tukaram, films about Mahatma Phule and Vasudev Balwant Phadke, Dhanya te Santaji Dhanaji and Bal Shivaji. It also lists Hindi films with historical backdrops, including Sikandar, Tansen, Samrat Chandragupta, Prithvivallabha and Mughal-e-Azam.

The Government of India created a special division for documentaries called the Films Division. According to the textbook, its documentaries were shown in cinema theatres before the main movie and aimed to create public awareness.

Film studios

The chapter identifies Bombay Talkies, Rajkamal Productions, R. K. Studios and Navketan as studios that played significant roles in the development of the Indian film industry.

6.5 Entertainment and Professional Opportunities

The textbook explains that students of history can find professional opportunities in drama and cinema.

Drama and theatre

  • Accurate stage backdrops require knowledge of the art and architecture of the period being represented.
  • Historical experts may work as art directors or consultants.
  • Script and dialogue writers may need language experts, historians of language and cultural historians to represent the period appropriately.

Cinema

  • Art directors design settings, costumes, jewellery, make-up and hairstyles suitable to the time in which a film is set.
  • Historians can work as art directors or consultants to art directors.
  • Writing period-appropriate dialogue requires knowledge of the culture and language of the time.

3. Write notes

(1) Need of entertainment:
Entertainment refreshes the mind, gives happiness and may increase physical energy and work efficiency. Hobbies and games support personality development. The textbook distinguishes active entertainment, which needs mental and physical participation, from passive entertainment, in which people watch or listen.

(2) Marathi Theatre:
The textbook traces Marathi theatre to the Dashavatara tradition. Vishnudas Bhave is described as its pioneer and father. Early performances often used spontaneous dialogue. V. J. Kirtane wrote the script for Thorale Madhavrao Peshwe in 1861. Later, classical music helped musical theatre flourish, with plays such as Sangeet Shakuntal and Sharada.

(3) Entertainment and professional opportunities:
Students of history can work as consultants or art directors for theatre and cinema. Historical knowledge helps recreate period-appropriate settings, architecture, costumes, jewellery, hairstyles and language. Script and dialogue writers may also need advice from historians and language experts.

Historical films need accurate settings, architecture, costumes, jewellery, make-up, hairstyles and language appropriate to the period. Historians can guide art directors and dialogue writers so the presentation reflects the intended time.

The textbook explains that Eknath’s Bharuds have a wide range of subjects, dramatic quality, easy rhythm and humour. They communicate spiritual, ethical and practical lessons about life.
