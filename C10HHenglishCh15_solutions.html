Complete Solutions / Study Notes

1. Chapter Overview

“Let’s March!” is based on the Nobel Acceptance Speech of Kailash Satyarthi. The speech focuses on the freedom, dignity, education, safety and dreams of children. The speaker draws attention to children affected by child labour, slavery, violence, trafficking, exploitation and lack of education. He calls upon individuals, governments, organisations, teachers, businesses and society to act with compassion and urgency.

The existing solution notes describe the speech as an appeal to unite, globalise compassion and take collective action so that every child can grow, learn and dream. fileciteturn184file1L21-L35

Central message: Every child matters. Real change requires compassion, education, courage and collective action. The closing call is to “march” from exploitation to education, poverty to shared prosperity, slavery to liberty and violence to peace.

2. Warming Up — Chit-Chat

The textbook asks students to compare two situations, decide which situation should change, discuss how and when change can happen, and explain how students can help needy children. fileciteturn184file0L3-L13

Model Answers

What difference do you notice?
One situation shows children who are deprived of basic opportunities such as education and a safe childhood, while the other represents children enjoying the opportunities and care they deserve.

Which situation should change?
The situation in which children are deprived of education, safety, freedom and a proper childhood should change.

How and when can the situation change?
It can change through awareness, education, responsible action, support from society and effective implementation of child-protection measures. Change should begin immediately.

How can a student help?
A student can spread awareness, respect every child, support educational initiatives, avoid encouraging child labour, inform responsible adults when a child is in danger, and participate in suitable school/community activities.

3. Speech — Part I: Main Ideas

Satyarthi begins by addressing the audience and expressing honour at the platform of peace and humanity. He recites a mantra from the ancient Vedas and presents it as a prayer, aspiration and resolve capable of helping humanity overcome man-made crises. He calls for global progress in which no person is left behind. fileciteturn184file0L41-L60

Tribute and Nobel Honour

He recalls freeing children from slavery and says that the smiles of freed children represent a profound reward. He gives credit to people associated with the movement, including Kaalu Kumar, Dhoom Das, Adarsh Kishore and Iqbal Masih, and accepts the honour on behalf of martyrs, fellow activists and his countrymen. fileciteturn184file0L61-L75

“Sound of Silence” and “Face of Invisibility”

Satyarthi says he represents the sound of silence, the cry of innocence and the face of invisibility. He keeps an empty chair as a reminder of millions of children who are left behind. fileciteturn184file0L93-L103

Child Labourer and Child Soldier

He recalls meeting a child labourer in the foothills of the Himalayas who asked why the world could not give him a toy and a book instead of forcing him to take a gun or a tool. He also recalls a Sudanese child-soldier who was kidnapped by an extremist militia and forced, as his first training lesson, to kill his friends and family. fileciteturn184file0L104-L117

What Satyarthi Refuses to Accept

  • He refuses to accept that places of worship have no place for children's dreams.
  • He refuses to accept that the world is too poor to bring children to classrooms.
  • He refuses to accept that laws, constitutions, police and judges are unable to protect children.
  • He refuses to accept that slavery can be stronger than the quest for freedom.

These refusals form a strong repeated section of Part I. fileciteturn184file0L118-L131

4. Speech — Part I Continued

His Aim in Life

His aim is that every child should be free to be a child — free to grow and develop, eat, sleep, see daylight, laugh, cry, play, learn, go to school and, above all, dream.

Education and Global Citizenship

The speaker identifies fear and intolerance as a major challenge. He argues that education should provide meaning and purpose in life and develop a sense of global citizenship among young people. He connects rights, security and hope with education. fileciteturn184file0L169-L181

The “Daughters”

He refers to Malala, Kayanat, another Kayanat, Shazia and daughters from Africa and around the world as young people who are choosing peace over violence, tolerance over extremism and courage over fear. fileciteturn184file0L182-L200

5. Speech — Part II: “We Can Do It …”

The Tiny Bird and the Forest Fire

Satyarthi tells the story of a forest fire. While animals flee, a tiny bird carries one drop of water in its beak to help extinguish the fire. When the lion questions how such a small effort can help, the bird replies that she is “doing my bit.”

Lesson: Individual effort matters. A person should not avoid action merely because the problem is large. Small contributions can become powerful when many people act together.

The forest-fire story and the bird's response are given in Part II on page 109. fileciteturn184file0L211-L228

Globalisation and Compassion

The speech observes that people are connected through high-speed Internet, global markets and air travel, but there can still be a serious lack of compassion. Satyarthi calls for transforming individual compassion into global compassion. fileciteturn184file0L229-L237

“They Are All Our Children”

He asks whose children stitch footballs without playing with them, harvest cocoa without tasting chocolate, suffer disease, or are kidnapped and held hostage. His answer is: they are all our children.

6. The Eight-Year-Old Girl and the Call to Action

Satyarthi recalls an eight-year-old girl rescued from intergenerational forced labour in stone quarries. Immediately after rescue, she asked, “Why did you not come earlier?” Her question makes him reflect on society's inaction and the need for urgent collective action. fileciteturn184file0L268-L283

What Matters?

Every single minute matters, every single child matters, and every single childhood matters.

What Does He Challenge?

He challenges passivity and pessimism surrounding children, the culture of silence, passivity and neutrality. He calls on governments, agencies, businesses, faith leaders, workers, teachers, NGOs and individuals to end violence against children, including slavery, trafficking, child marriage, child labour, sexual abuse and illiteracy. fileciteturn184file0L284-L316

7. Tomorrow Becomes TODAY

As a child, Satyarthi saw a cobbler boy of his own age sitting outside the school. When he asked why the boy was not in school, the boy's father said that they were born to work. Satyarthi says the answer made him angry and still does. His childhood vision was to see that boy sitting beside him in the classroom. He declares that this tomorrow has become TODAY. fileciteturn184file0L329-L346

10. Language Study

1(A). Active and Passive Voice

Part A — PassivePart B — Active
(1) He was kidnapped by an extremist militia.(b) An extremist militia kidnapped him.
(2) The child was forced to kill his friends and family.(a) They forced the child to kill his friends and family.

Answer: (1) → (b), (2) → (a).

In the passive structure, the receiver of the action is placed first and the agent may follow “by”. In the active structure, the doer performs the action directly.

11. Types of Sentences

The textbook defines a sentence as a group of words expressing a complete idea and introduces four types. fileciteturn184file0L567-L584

TypeMeaningExample
Assertive / StatementMakes a statement or assertion.There are many villages in India.
InterrogativeAsks a question.What is the time?
ImperativeExpresses a command or request.Open your books.
ExclamatoryExpresses strong feeling.What a shame!

Interrogative Sub-types

  • Yes/No Interrogative: Generally begins with a verb and can be answered with “Yes” or “No”. Example: “Are you coming with us?”
  • Wh-Interrogative: Begins with a Wh-word such as “who” or “why” and cannot normally be answered with only yes/no. Example: “What is the time?”

12. Important Exam Questions

Q1. Why does Satyarthi keep an empty chair on the podium?

The empty chair is a reminder of millions of children who are left behind and whose voices, dreams and suffering are often ignored.

Q2. What is Satyarthi's only aim in life?

His aim is that every child should be free to be a child, grow and develop, eat, sleep, laugh, cry, play, learn, attend school and dream.

Q3. What does the story of the tiny bird teach?

It teaches that every individual can contribute. Even a small effort matters, and collective small efforts can create significant change.

Q4. Why does the question of the eight-year-old girl “Why did you not come earlier?” affect Satyarthi?

It forces him and society to confront the delay and inaction that allow children to remain in exploitation. The question becomes a challenge to collective responsibility and urgency.

Q5. Explain the significance of “TODAY”.

For Satyarthi, the childhood vision of a poor working boy sitting beside him in school should no longer remain a future dream. He declares that the future must become present action: every child should have rights to life, freedom, health, education, safety, dignity, equality and peace.

Q6. What does “Let us march” mean in the speech?

It is a call for positive social transformation: moving from exploitation towards education, poverty towards shared prosperity, slavery towards liberty, violence towards peace, ignorance towards awakening and darkness towards light.
