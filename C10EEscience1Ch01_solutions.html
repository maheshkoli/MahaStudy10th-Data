Chapter at a Glance

This chapter explains gravitation as a universal force and describes how it keeps objects falling toward Earth and planets moving in their orbits. The main topics are circular motion and centripetal force, Kepler’s laws, Newton’s universal law of gravitation, acceleration due to gravity, mass and weight, free fall, escape velocity, and weightlessness.

Important Concepts Explained

Gravitation

Gravitation is the attractive force between any two objects in the universe. The textbook introduces Newton’s idea that Earth attracts an apple toward Earth’s centre.

Circular motion and centripetal force

An object moving in a circle continually changes direction, so a force must act toward the centre. This is called centripetal force. If the force on a stone tied to a string stops, the stone moves along the tangent at the release point.

Kepler’s laws

  1. First law: A planet’s orbit is an ellipse, with the Sun at one focus.
  2. Second law: The line joining a planet and the Sun sweeps equal areas in equal intervals of time.
  3. Third law: The square of a planet’s period of revolution is proportional to the cube of its mean distance from the Sun: \(T^2/r^3=K\).

Newton’s universal law of gravitation

Every object attracts every other object. The force is proportional to the product of the masses and inversely proportional to the square of the distance between their centres.

\[F = G\\frac{m_1m_2}{d^2}\]

The textbook gives \(G = \(6.673\times10^{-11}\,\mathrm{N\,m^2\,kg^{-2}}\)\).

Acceleration due to gravity

The Earth’s gravitational force produces acceleration denoted by g, directed toward Earth’s centre.

\(g=\frac{GM}{R^2}\)

Using the Earth’s mass and radius given in the textbook, the chapter calculates a value of approximately 9.77 \(\mathrm{m/s^2}\); the quick revision section commonly rounds this to 9.8 \(\mathrm{m/s^2}\).

How g varies

  • Across Earth’s surface: the textbook states that g is highest at the poles (9.832 \(\mathrm{m/s^2}\)) and lowest at the equator (9.78 \(\mathrm{m/s^2}\)).
  • With height: g decreases as distance from Earth’s centre increases.
  • With depth: g decreases as we go deeper inside Earth, reaching zero at the centre.
  • On the Moon: g is about one-sixth of its value on Earth.

Free Fall

An object is in free fall when it moves under the influence of gravity alone. The textbook notes that air resistance and buoyancy act in ordinary air, so true free fall is possible only in a vacuum.

For an object released from rest, the textbook uses \(u=0\) and acceleration \(a=g\).

\[v=gt,\\qquad s=\\frac{1}{2}gt^2,\\qquad v^2=2gs\]

For an object thrown vertically upward, acceleration is opposite to the direction of motion and is taken as \(-g\) under the textbook’s sign convention.

Escape Velocity and Weightlessness

Escape velocity is the minimum speed needed for an object to escape Earth’s gravitational influence, according to the supplied solution notes.

\[v_{\\text{esc}}=\\sqrt{\\frac{2GM}{R}}=\\sqrt{2gR}\\approx 11.2\\;\\text{km/s}\]

Definitions and Key Terms

Centripetal force
Force directed toward the centre of a circular path.
Ellipse
A curve with two foci; the sum of the distances from any point on the ellipse to the two foci is constant.
Universal gravitational constant (G)
The constant of proportionality in Newton’s universal law of gravitation.
Centre of mass
The point at which the total mass of an object can be assumed to be concentrated.
Acceleration due to gravity (g)
Acceleration produced by gravitational force.
Free fall
Motion under the influence of gravity alone.
Escape velocity
Minimum velocity needed to escape a celestial body’s gravitational influence.

Worked Example 1 — Force between two people

Question: Mahendra and Virat are 1 m apart. Their masses are 75 kg and 80 kg. Find the gravitational force between them.

Given: \(d=1 m\), \(\(m_1\)=75 kg\), \(\(m_2\)=80 kg\), \(G=\(6.67\times10^{-11}\,\mathrm{N\,m^2/kg^2}\)\).

  1. Use \(F=G\(m_1\)\(m_2\)/d²\).
  2. Substitute: \(F=(6.67 × 10⁻¹¹ × 75 × 80)/1²\).
  3. Calculate: \(F=4.002 × \(10^{-7}\) N\).

Answer: 4.002 × \(10^{-7}\) N.

Worked Example 2 — Velocity after one second

Question: Using the force above, estimate Mahendra’s velocity after 1 s, assuming a frictionless bench and constant acceleration.

Given: \(F=4.002 × \(10^{-7}\) N\), \(m=75 kg\), \(u=0\), \(t=1 s\).

  1. Acceleration: \(a=F/m=(4.002 × \(10^{-7}\))/75=5.34 × \(10^{-9}\) \(\mathrm{m/s^2}\)\).
  2. Use \(v=u+at\).
  3. \(v=0+(5.34 × \(10^{-9}\) × 1)=5.34 × \(10^{-9}\) m/s\).

Answer: 5.34 × \(10^{-9}\) m/s, under the stated assumptions.

Worked Example 3 — Earth’s force on a person

Question: Calculate the gravitational force of Earth on a 75 kg person.

Given: \(M=6 × 10²⁴ kg\), \(R=6.4 × 10⁶ m\), \(m=75 kg\), \(G=\(6.67\times10^{-11}\,\mathrm{N\,m^2/kg^2}\)\).

Use \(F=GMm/R²\).

\(F=(6.67 × 10⁻¹¹ × 6 × 10²⁴ × 75)/(6.4 × 10⁶)²\).

Answer: approximately 733 N, as calculated in the textbook.

Worked Example 4 — Free fall from 125 m

Question: An iron ball is released from 125 m. Take \(g=10 \(\mathrm{m/s^2}\)\). Find the time to reach the ground and the velocity just before impact.

  1. Use \(s=½gt²\): \(125=5t²\), so \(t²=25\) and \(t=5 s\).
  2. Use \(v=gt=10 × 5=50 m/s\).
  3. At half the total time, \(t=2.5 s\). Distance fallen is \(s=½ × 10 × 2.5²=31.25 m\), so height above the ground is \(125-31.25=93.75 m\).

Answers: 5 s; 50 m/s downward; 93.75 m above ground at half-time.

Practice Set — Selected Answers

Mass and weight

Mass is the amount of matter and is measured in kilograms; weight is gravitational force and is measured in newtons. Mass remains constant, while weight changes with local gravity.

Why is g zero at Earth’s centre?

At the centre, gravitational pulls from all directions balance, so the net gravitational force and g are zero.

Kepler’s third law

If the orbital radius changes from \(R\) to \(2R\), then \(T₂²/T₁²=(2R)³/R³=8\), hence \(T₂=√8T₁\).

Moon weight

The supplied solution notes state that weight on the Moon is approximately one-sixth of weight on Earth. For an Earth weight of 49 N, the Moon weight is about \(49/6=8.17 N\).

Important Questions and Answers

Why does a stone fall toward Earth?

Earth attracts the stone through gravitational force, directed toward Earth’s centre.

Why does a planet remain in orbit?

The Sun’s gravitational attraction supplies the centripetal force that continually changes the planet’s direction of motion.

Why does the Earth not visibly move toward a falling apple?

The Earth and apple exert equal gravitational forces on each other, but Earth’s enormous mass makes its acceleration extremely small.

Why is weight different on the Moon?

Weight depends on \(g\). The textbook states that the Moon’s gravitational acceleration is about one-sixth of Earth’s.

Why is true free fall associated with vacuum?

In ordinary air, air resistance and buoyant force also act on a falling object. In a vacuum, these effects are absent.

Common Errors and Quick Revision

  • Do not confuse mass (kg) with weight (N).
  • Use metres, kilograms and seconds when substituting into SI formulas.
  • Use the square of distance in Newton’s gravitation formula.
  • Keep track of the direction/sign of acceleration in upward and downward motion.
  • For Kepler’s third law, remember \(\(T^2\) ∝ \(r^3\)\), not \(T ∝ \(r^3\)\).
  • In free-fall questions, identify the initial velocity and the direction chosen as positive.
