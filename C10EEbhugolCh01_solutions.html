FIELD VISIT

Proper Study Notes · Key Concepts · Solved Textbook Exercise · Exam Revision

Based on the uploaded eight-page textbook chapter.

How to use these notes

Learn the key terms, understand the route-wise observations, and practise the five textbook exercise questions. The sample report is a model: replace its details with observations from your own field visit. The chapter states that questions should not be based on this exact sample trip; questions may be asked about field visits in general.

1. Chapter at a Glance

Core idea

A field visit is a practical way to study geography by observing natural and human-made features directly and recording information.

Textbook journey

Rahul and his classmates travel with their teachers from Naldurg in Osmanabad district to Alibag in Raigad district.

What students observe

Relief, water bodies, vegetation, soil, agriculture, human settlements, settlement patterns, occupations, weather and coastal landforms.

Skills developed

Observation, map reading, asking questions, collecting information, photography, note-making and preparing a report.

2. Route and Changing Landscapes

Naldurg

The students observe undulating topography, some plains, agricultural fields, small settlements and semi-arid thorny vegetation. Jujube (bor) and babool trees are mentioned.

Rural settlements

Some houses stand in a straight line along roads. The textbook calls this a linear settlement. Traditional mud-and-wood houses are also shown.

Solapur region

The students observe an urban area with high population density, multi-storeyed houses, shops, shopping malls and restaurants. Pulses such as moog and urad are seen around the region.

Ujani reservoir

Near Indapur, the students observe the backwaters of the Ujani dam on the Bhima River. The textbook lists drinking-water supply, power generation, fishing and irrigation among its uses.

Pune and Sinhagad

The landscape becomes hillier and more rugged, with hills, valleys, rock pinnacles, rock layers and landslide debris. Basalt is identified as an igneous rock. Paddy is seen in areas receiving good rainfall.

Western Ghats

The chapter describes gentle slopes to the east and steep slopes to the west, with cliffs and waterfalls. The Bhor or Khandala Ghat is crossed towards the western coastal plains.

Western coastal plains

Students see dense, broad-leaved vegetation in the Ghats, teak and deciduous trees, then sparser forest, paddy fields and industrial estates. Near the sea, humidity and rainfall are high; rice is a major crop.

Alibag and Kolaba Fort

Students visit the beach and Kolaba/Alibag Fort. The textbook describes it as a sea-fort built on a wave-cut platform and surrounded by seawater. Tide timings must be considered.

3. Important Concepts and Definitions

Field visit

A planned visit to observe geographical features, collect first-hand information and understand relationships between environment and human activities.

Relief / topography

The shape and physical features of land, such as plains, slopes, hills, valleys and cliffs.

Linear settlement

A settlement in which houses are arranged in a line, often along a road.

Dry deciduous vegetation

Vegetation whose trees shed their leaves during a particular season.

Semi-arid thorny vegetation

Vegetation found in relatively dry areas, including thorny shrubs and trees.

Irrigation

Supplying water to fields artificially. In the chapter, irrigation facilities help explain the shift from pulses to sugarcane.

Reservoir

Stored water, often associated with a dam. The chapter discusses Ujani and Khadakwasla reservoirs.

Basalt

An igneous rock identified by Rahul in the Sinhagad region.

Wave-cut platform

A coastal rock surface formed by sea-wave action; the textbook uses this term to explain the setting of Kolaba Fort.

Primary occupations

Activities directly dependent on natural resources. The chapter identifies fishing and agriculture as primary occupations.

Horticultural farming

Cultivation of fruits and similar crops. The chapter mentions coconut, betelnut, jackfruit, banana and some spices.

Devrai

A sacred grove; the chapter also mentions vanrais (woodlands) and devrais.

4. Geography Connections to Remember

Vegetation and rainfall

The textbook states that a change in vegetation indicates a change in rainfall. Dry thorny vegetation is observed near Naldurg, while denser vegetation appears in wetter parts of the route.

Rainfall, irrigation and crops

The students observe pulses near Naldurg, sugarcane where irrigation facilities are available, and paddy in areas with good rainfall. These are observations from the textbook route, not a rule that every region must follow.

Relief and settlement

The journey shows changes from undulating plains to rugged hills and then coastal plains. Settlement density and building types also vary, with more multi-storeyed buildings in urban Solapur.

Coastal climate

As the group approaches the sea, students notice increased humidity and perspiration. The teacher links this to nearness to the sea and high rainfall.

Landforms made by sea waves

The students recall beaches, sea caves, wave-cut platforms and sand bars.

Forts and location

Naldurg is described as a fort on land, Sinhagad as a hill fort, and Kolaba/Alibag as a sea-fort.

5. Field-Visit Preparation and Safety

Items mentioned

I-card, notebook, pen, binoculars, camera, cap, map and water bottle. Students also carry personal luggage; the illustrated kit includes a first-aid box.

Before the visit

Decide the objective, route, duration and places to observe. Prepare questions and carry necessary materials.

During the visit

Stay with the group, follow teachers’ instructions, observe both sides of the route, record notes, take relevant photographs and avoid disturbing the environment.

At the coast or a fort

Follow safety instructions. Check high- and low-tide timings before visiting a sea-fort away from the coast. Avoid unsafe water and restricted areas.

Waste management

Keep litter in a bag and put it in a designated bin. Do not throw wrappers or bottles on roads, from the bus or at the destination.

After the visit

Organize observations and photographs, compare the places visited and prepare a report with a conclusion.

6. Solved Textbook Exercise — Answer in Short

Q1. Make a report on your field visit.

Sample report (adapt it to your own visit):
Title: Field Visit to a Local Geographical Area
Objective: To observe relief, water bodies, vegetation, soil, agriculture, settlements and occupations.
Route and places: Mention your actual starting point, route, places visited and date.
Observations: Record landforms, plants, crops, settlement patterns, water resources and local occupations at each stop. Add labelled photographs or sketches where possible.
Conclusion: The visit helped us understand how physical features, rainfall, water availability and human needs influence agriculture, settlements and occupations.

Q2. Prepare a questionnaire for a field visit to a factory.

1. What is the name and type of the factory?
2. When was it established?
3. What raw materials are used?
4. What products are manufactured?
5. Where do the raw materials come from?
6. What machinery and energy sources are used?
7. How many people work here and what jobs do they perform?
8. Where are the products sold or transported?
9. How are water use and waste managed?
10. What safety measures are followed by workers?

Q3. How will you manage litter during the field visit?

I will carry a separate bag for waste, keep wrappers and bottles in it, and dispose of waste in designated bins. I will not throw litter from the bus or leave it at the visiting place. I will encourage classmates to keep the area clean.

Q4. What items will you take with you for the field visit?

I will carry my I-card, notebook, pen or pencil, map, water bottle, cap, camera and binoculars if available. A first-aid kit should be available for the group. I will carry only necessary personal luggage and follow the teacher’s instructions.

Q5. Outline the importance of a field visit.

A field visit gives first-hand experience of geographical features. It develops observation, map-reading, questioning, data-collection and reporting skills. It connects classroom learning with real places and helps students understand how relief, rainfall, water, vegetation and human activities are related.

7. Additional Exam-Oriented Questions

Q1. Why did crops change along the route?

The textbook records pulses near Naldurg, sugarcane in an area with irrigation facilities, and paddy in a region with good rainfall. Availability of irrigation and rainfall helps explain these observed changes.

Q2. What is a linear settlement?

It is a settlement in which houses are arranged in a line, often along a road.

Q3. What uses of the Ujani dam are mentioned?

The textbook mentions drinking-water supply, power generation, fishing and irrigation.

Q4. Distinguish between the three forts described.

Naldurg is a fort on land; Sinhagad is a hill fort built on a hill; Kolaba/Alibag is a sea-fort built on a wave-cut platform and surrounded by seawater.

Q5. Why check tide timings before visiting Kolaba Fort?

The fort is away from the coast and surrounded by seawater, so access depends on high and low tides.

Q6. What information can be collected at the Talathi office?

The chapter suggests asking about crops, soil types, fruit and cash-crop cultivation, land revenue, irrigated land, watershed programmes and other village occupations.

Q7. Which occupations are mentioned for the coastal region?

Fishing and agriculture are identified as primary occupations. Horticultural crops and tourism are also mentioned as important activities.

Q8. How can information be collected during a field visit?

By observing features, reading information boards and maps, asking prepared questions, interviewing relevant people, taking notes and photographs, and consulting local offices where appropriate.
