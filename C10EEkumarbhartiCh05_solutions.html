1. Chapter at a Glance

This chapter introduces students to the historical figure Joan of Arc through an extract from the play by George Bernard Shaw. The chapter focuses on:

  • Understanding the historical context of the Hundred Years' War between England and France.
  • Analyzing character traits through dialogue and action.
  • Developing language skills, including the use of phrasal verbs and word forms.
  • Practicing formal letter writing and interview techniques.

2. Important Concepts Explained

  • Historical Context: The play is set in 1429, during the Hundred Years' War (1337–1453). Northern France was under foreign control (English and Burgundians). Joan of Arc, a peasant girl, led the French army to several victories.
  • Character Analysis:
  • Joan of Arc: A confident, well-built, strong country girl (17–18 years old) with immense faith. She believes God is on her side.
  • Captain Robert de Baudricourt: A military officer who is initially skeptical and dismissive of Joan but is eventually persuaded by her conviction.
  • Bertrand de Poulengey (Polly): A French guard who believes in Joan and convinces Robert to give her a chance.
  • Thematic Elements: The play explores themes of faith, conviction, gender roles (breaking stereotypes), and the power of belief to inspire others.

3. Definitions and Key Terms

  • Grimly: Seriously or in a gloomy manner.
  • Squire: A person of high rank (often a knight's attendant).
  • Armour: Protective clothing of metal or leather worn in battles.
  • Blockhead: A stupid person.
  • Dauphin: The oldest son of the King of France, the heir to the throne.
  • Monsieur: French word for "Mr."
  • Assuming: Taking for granted.
  • Retreats hastily: Moves away in a hurry.
  • Chinon: A city in France where the Royal family resided during the war.
  • Cowed: Frightened.
  • Wavering: Hesitating.
  • Have put fire into me: Have inspired or motivated me.
  • Obstinately: In a stubborn and firm manner.
  • Gravely: Seriously.
  • Raising a siege: Surrounding a place from all sides to attack and remove the forces defending it.
  • Plundering: Looting.
  • To save their skins: To save their lives.
  • Let come what may: Let anything happen.
  • Wash one's hands off it: Not take any responsibility for it.

4. Formulas, Rules, and Properties

  • Phrasal Verbs: A phrasal verb is a verb made up of a main verb together with an adverb, a preposition, or both (e.g., "call out," "look into"). Adding different prepositions to the same verb changes its meaning.

2(B). Character sketch of Joan of Arc

Title: Joan of Arc — A Courageous Patriot

Joan of Arc is a confident, courageous and determined young French country girl. Although she is only about seventeen or eighteen, she has a clear mission: to meet the Dauphin and help free Orléans from English control. She is not frightened by Captain Robert’s anger or authority. Her strong faith makes her believe that God will help her succeed. Joan is practical too: she asks only for a horse, suitable armour and a few soldiers. She believes that French soldiers can win if they fight for their country instead of merely trying to save themselves. Her patriotism, optimism and ability to inspire others make her an extraordinary leader.

3. Comparisons from the extract

  1. As easy as: Chasing a cow out of a meadow — Robert uses this comparison to suggest that Joan wrongly assumes raising a siege is easy.
  2. As mad as: Joan — Robert calls Poulengey as mad as Joan because he also supports her unusual plan.
  3. The Dauphin in Chinon is like: “a rat in a corner,” because he is trapped, frightened and unwilling to fight.
  4. The enemy soldiers will be driven away like: sheep, according to Joan’s confident prediction.
  5. Joan of Arc is a bit of: a miracle, in Poulengey’s opinion.

4. Say why

(a) Joan wanted to meet Captain Squire Robert.
She wanted him to provide a horse, armour and a few soldiers so that she could go to the Dauphin and help free Orléans.

(b) Joan did not ask for many soldiers.
She believed that the Dauphin would provide what she needed and that a small escort would be enough to take her to him. She also believed that her faith and leadership could inspire the French army.

(c) Poulengey, Jack and Dick offered to accompany Joan.
Poulengey felt there was something special about Joan and believed that helping her was the French side’s last chance. Jack and Dick were willing to go with her.

(d) French soldiers were always beaten in war.
Joan says they fought only to save their own lives, so they ran away rather than fighting for France. She believed patriotism and courage would change their behaviour.

(e) Robert said, “I wash my hands off it.”
He meant that he would not take responsibility for what happened after Joan left with the escort and soldier’s dress.

6. Compare Joan of Arc with Rani of Jhansi

Similarities

  1. Both were women who took courageous action during a conflict to defend their homeland.
  2. Both became symbols of bravery and patriotism.
  3. Both inspired others to resist a stronger opposing force.

Contrasts

  1. Joan was a French peasant girl who said her mission was guided by God; Rani Lakshmibai was a queen and ruler of Jhansi.
  2. Joan’s role in this extract is to persuade military officers to send her to the Dauphin; Rani Lakshmibai led forces during the Indian Rebellion of 1857.
  3. Joan’s mission in the play focuses on helping free Orléans and supporting the French crown; Rani Lakshmibai fought against British rule in India and defended Jhansi.

7. Summary in indirect speech (15–20 lines)

Joan greeted Captain Robert de Baudricourt and asked him to give her a horse, armour and some soldiers. She told him that she wanted to meet the Dauphin and help free Orléans. Robert thought that she was mad and asked why she had not been sent back to her father. Joan replied that she had already come despite Robert’s earlier refusal to see her. She explained that a horse would cost sixteen francs and that she could save money by using a soldier’s armour. She said that she would not need many soldiers because the Dauphin would give her what she needed to free Orléans. She named the men who had promised to accompany her, including Jack and Polly. She also mentioned John Godsave, Dick the Archer, John of Honecourt and Julian. Joan said that she had arranged everything and that Robert only needed to give the order. Robert was surprised by her confidence and asked the Steward whether Monsieur de Poulengey really wished to go with her. The Steward confirmed that Poulengey and Monsieur de Metz wanted to accompany her. Joan remained confident that her mission would succeed.

8. Important Questions and Answers

  • Q: Why did Robert initially think Joan was mad?
  • A: Robert thought she was mad because she was a young peasant girl asking for soldiers and armour to fight a war, which was considered impossible for someone of her background and gender at that time.
  • Q: How did Joan change Robert's mind?
  • A: Joan showed immense faith and confidence. She convinced him that she was not afraid, that God was on her side, and that she could inspire the French soldiers to stop running away and start fighting.

9. Common Errors and How to Avoid Them

  • Confusing Phrasal Verbs: Students often confuse the meanings of phrasal verbs like "look into" and "look for." Always check the context; "into" implies investigation, while "for" implies searching.
  • Indirect Speech: When converting "Please come" to indirect speech, remember to use "requested" or "begged" instead of just "said."
