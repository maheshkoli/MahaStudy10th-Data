  • Describe the development of the party system in India.

  • Explain regionalism and the role of regional parties.

Meaning and Characteristics of Political Parties

A political party is a group of people who aim to contest elections, win elections, acquire political power and establish a government. Political parties are social organisations, but their goal and way of working differ from those of other organisations because they participate directly in the electoral process to gain political power.

Main characteristics

  1. To be in power: Parties compete in elections to acquire political power. The textbook says this competition is acceptable when it is fair.
  2. To pursue an ideology: Each party has policies, views and positions on social issues. Together these form its ideology. People who accept a party’s ideology may support it; this support is called its mass base.
  3. To have a party agenda: Parties prepare an agenda based on their ideology and try to implement it if they come to power. Even out of power, they seek public support for the agenda.
  4. To establish a government: The party or parties that obtain a majority form the government; parties outside the government act as opposition.
  5. To link government and people: Parties communicate people’s demands and complaints to the government and help the government seek public support for its policies and programmes.

Changing Party System in India

The textbook describes three broad developments in the party system:

  1. Single dominant-party era: After independence, Congress was strong at the Centre and in most states. This period is described as the era of the “single dominant party system.”
  2. Challenge in 1977: Non-Congress parties came together and challenged the single dominant-party system.
  3. Coalition politics after 1989: The textbook says the 1989 Lok Sabha elections marked the end of the system in which one party emerged as dominant. Different parties then increasingly joined to form coalition governments. It notes that both the Bharatiya Janata Party and Congress experimented with coalition governments and that coalition politics became established in India.

Types of party systems

  • One-party system: One party retains power for a long period and other parties are not influential.
  • Two-party system: Two parties are influential and alternate in power.
  • Multi-party system: Several parties compete for power and have comparable influence.

The textbook also mentions the idea of partyless democracy associated with Mahatma Gandhi, Vinoba Bhave and Jayaprakash Narayan, and invites students to think about how such a system might work today.

National and Regional Party Recognition

The textbook gives criteria for recognition of national and regional parties, citing an Election Commission notification dated 13 December 2016. These are reproduced here as historical textbook content, not as a statement of current recognition rules.

National party criteria shown in the textbook

  • Secure at least 6% of valid votes in four or more states in earlier Lok Sabha or State Assembly elections, and have at least four Lok Sabha members elected; or
  • Have candidates elected from at least 2% of the total Lok Sabha constituencies, across at least three states.

Regional party criteria shown in the textbook

  • Secure at least 6% of valid votes in earlier Lok Sabha or State Assembly elections and have at least two members elected to a State Legislative Assembly; or
  • Secure at least 3% of seats in the State Legislative Assembly or at least three seats.

Study tip: These thresholds are presented exactly as the chapter’s cited 2016 reference. Election Commission recognition rules can change, so use a current official source if you need present-day legal criteria.

National Parties Described in the Textbook

The textbook introduces the following parties and summarises their history or stated principles. This section follows the textbook’s framing and examples.

Indian National Congress (INC)

Established in 1885, Congress began as a broad nationalist movement. The textbook describes its emphasis on secularism, development, equal rights, minority welfare, democratic socialism, social equality and international peace.

Communist Party of India (CPI)

The textbook dates its establishment to 1925 and describes its communist ideology, focus on workers and labourers, and opposition to capitalism. It notes that differences in 1962 led to a split and the formation of CPI (Marxist).

Bharatiya Janata Party (BJP)

The textbook says the BJP was established in 1980 after the Janata Party split; Bharatiya Jana Sangh had been established in 1951. It describes the party as supporting the protection of Indian culture and traditions and giving importance to economic reforms.

Communist Party of India (Marxist), CPI(M)

The textbook describes CPI(M) as advocating socialism, secularism and democracy, opposing imperialism, and protecting the interests of workers, farmers and landless labourers.

Bahujan Samaj Party (BSP)

Established in 1984, the textbook says the BSP aims to protect the interests of the “majority,” including Scheduled Castes, Scheduled Tribes, religious minorities and Other Backward Classes.

Nationalist Congress Party (NCP)

The textbook says the NCP was formed in 1999 following a split in the Congress Party. It describes the party as believing in democracy, equality and secularism.

All India Trinamool Congress (AITC)

The textbook dates its establishment to 1998 and describes its support for democracy, secularism and protection of weaker sections of society. The chapter says it received national-party recognition in 2016, reflecting the reference period used by the book.

Regionalism and Regional Parties

India has many languages, cultures, traditions and geographical regions. The textbook explains that attachment to a language or region can develop into regional identity and regionalism. When linguistic, regional and cultural identities combine, regional consciousness may become stronger and lead to political parties, pressure groups or movements that seek to protect regional interests.

Regional parties are parties that represent a particular region’s identity and seek political power with the objective of regional development. Their influence may be concentrated in one region, but they can also influence national politics, especially through coalition governments.

Common demands described in the chapter

  • Resolve regional problems at the regional level.
  • Give people from the region a role in political power.
  • Give residents of the region preference in administration and employment.
  • Demand more autonomy and authority while cooperating with the federal government.

Changing role of regional parties

The textbook describes a broad movement from separatist demands in some regions, to demands for autonomy, to seeking power at state and national levels and participation in mainstream national politics. It identifies coalition politics as one consequence of the growing influence of regional parties.

Major Regional Parties in the Textbook

Shiv Sena

The textbook identifies Shiv Sena as a major political party in Maharashtra, established in 1966. It describes its objectives as protecting the rights of Marathi people and promoting the Marathi language. The chapter refers to its coalition with the BJP in Maharashtra in the historical period covered by the textbook.

Shiromani Akali Dal

The textbook dates the party to 1920 and describes it as a prominent regional party in Punjab with an emphasis on religious and regional identity.

Jammu and Kashmir National Conference

The textbook says it was established in 1932 and describes its objectives as protecting the interests of Kashmiri people and the region’s autonomous status.

Assam Gana Parishad

The chapter connects the party to the Assam Agreement of 1985. It describes its objectives as addressing problems of displaced people, protecting Assam’s cultural, linguistic and social identity, and promoting economic development.

Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam (DMK)

The textbook describes the DMK’s historical development from Dravidian political movements and identifies protection of Tamil identity as a key objective. It notes that the party participated in coalition government at the Centre for some years.

Textbook Exercise Solutions

1. Choose the correct option

(1) When people come together and participate in the electoral process to acquire political power, such organisations are called ______.
Answer: (c) Political parties.

(2) National Conference is a party in ______ State.
Answer: (d) Jammu and Kashmir.

(3) Justice Party — a non-Brahmin movement — was transformed into ______ political party.
Answer: (c) Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam.

2. True or false, with reasons

(1) Political parties act as a link between government and people.
Answer: True. The textbook explains that parties communicate people’s demands and complaints to government and help government seek support for policies and programmes.

(2) Political parties are social organisations.
Answer: True. They are groups of people organised around political objectives, but they differ from other social organisations because they contest elections to acquire political power.

(3) Coalition politics leads to instability.
Answer: False, according to the textbook. The chapter says the belief that coalition governments necessarily bring instability was proved wrong and that coalition government became established in India.

(4) Shiromani Akali Dal is a national party.
Answer: False, according to the chapter’s classification. The textbook presents Shiromani Akali Dal as a regional party in Punjab.

3. Explain the concepts

(1) Regionalism:
Regionalism is the development of a strong sense of identity and attachment to a region, its language, culture, traditions and interests. The textbook explains that this feeling may lead to political parties or movements that seek to protect regional interests.

(2) National parties:
National parties are political parties recognised at the national level under the criteria described by the Election Commission. The textbook’s criteria cite a 2016 notification and include vote-share and seat requirements. These are historical textbook criteria, not a guarantee of current rules.

5. Answer in brief

(1) What are the major characteristics of political parties?
Answer: Their main characteristics are: (1) seeking political power through elections, (2) pursuing an ideology, (3) preparing a party agenda, (4) establishing a government when they obtain a majority, and (5) acting as a link between government and people.

(2) What changes have taken place in the nature of political parties in India?
Answer: After independence, Congress was dominant at the Centre and in many states. In 1977, non-Congress parties came together to challenge the dominant-party system. After the 1989 Lok Sabha elections, coalition governments became more common and coalition politics became established. Regional parties also gained influence, increasingly seeking autonomy, regional development and participation in state and national governments.

Project work — suggested approach

Important Questions and Quick Revision

Important questions

Why are political parties important in a representative democracy?
They contest elections, offer agendas and ideologies, form governments or opposition, and communicate public demands and complaints to the government.

How do regional parties affect national politics?
Although their influence may be concentrated in a region, regional parties can influence national politics by participating in coalition governments and raising regional demands at the national level.

What is the difference between a national and regional party?
In the chapter, the distinction is connected to Election Commission recognition criteria and the geographical scale of political influence. The specific recognition thresholds shown are based on the textbook’s 2016 reference.

Quick revision sheet

  • Political party: A group that contests elections to acquire political power.
  • Mass base: Public support received by a party.
  • Opposition: Parties that do not form the government.
  • Party systems: One-party, two-party and multi-party systems.
  • 1977: Non-Congress parties challenged the dominant-party system.
  • After 1989: Coalition politics became established, according to the textbook.
  • Regionalism: Strong identification with a region and its interests.
  • Regional parties: Seek regional development and may influence national coalitions.
