1. Chapter at a Glance

Main idea

This chapter studies human settlement patterns in India and Brazil. It explains how climate, availability of water, slope and shape of the land, natural resources, transport and development affect where people live.

Topics covered

Nucleated and dispersed settlements; settlement patterns in India and Brazil; urbanisation; India's urbanisation trend from 1961 to 2011; Brazil's urbanisation trend from 1960 to 2010; and comparison of urban and rural settlements.

2. Important Definitions

Human settlement

A place where people establish homes and live, either in rural or urban communities.

Nucleated settlement

A settlement in which houses and buildings are clustered relatively close together.

Dispersed settlement

A settlement in which houses or small groups of houses are spread over a wide area.

Urbanisation

The level of urbanisation is measured as the percentage of the total population living in urban areas.

Urbanisation formula

Urbanisation level (%) = (Urban population ÷ Total population) × 100.

3. Settlement Patterns in India

Factors affecting settlement

The textbook identifies climatic conditions, availability of water, slope of the land and level of development as factors behind India's varied settlement patterns.

Nucleated settlements

The textbook locates nucleated settlements in cultivated parts of India, including the plateau region of the Narmada Valley, paddy lands in Bihar and Uttar Pradesh, and the Vindhyan Plateau.

Dispersed settlements

The textbook identifies dispersed settlements in tribal parts of central India, eastern and southern Rajasthan, Himalayan slopes and areas with dissected, uneven topography.

How to identify patterns

A nucleated pattern has houses clustered together. A dispersed pattern has houses separated by fields, hills, forests or other physical features. Use the textbook satellite images on page 46 to compare the patterns.

4. Settlement Patterns in Brazil

Eastern coastal areas

The textbook says early settlements were established mainly by European settlers in coastal areas. These settlements later became densely populated.

São Paulo

The chapter describes São Paulo as a nucleated settlement. It refers to rich soil suitable for coffee, nearby minerals such as iron ore, a steady energy supply and a good transport system as factors supporting development.

Amazon Basin and central Brazil

Settlements become sparse toward central Brazil. The textbook lists thick equatorial rainforests, climate described in the book as unhealthy for settlement, limitations on using natural resources and poor transport links as reasons.

North-east highlands

The textbook notes that rural areas in Brazil's north-east highlands suffer from extreme droughts, making agriculture difficult and settlements sparse.

Textbook activity

The table includes petrol pumps, theatres, weekly markets, primary health centres, police outposts, art galleries, Gram Panchayats, APMC markets, primary and senior schools, colleges, shops, multi-speciality hospitals, metro stations, bus stations and universities.

How to answer

Availability varies from place to place. For an exam activity, classify each amenity according to your locality and explain with local examples. Do not assume every village or every city has exactly the same facilities.

General study guide

Gram Panchayats are associated with village local governance. Metro stations, universities, art galleries and multi-speciality hospitals are commonly associated with urban centres. Weekly markets, shops, schools, health centres and bus services may occur in both rural and urban areas.

6. Urbanisation in India

Measurement

The level of urbanisation is the percentage of the total population living in urban areas.

Trend in Figure 7.5

The textbook graph shows: 1961 — 18.0%; 1971 — 18.2%; 1981 — 23.3%; 1991 — 25.7%; 2001 — 27.8%; 2011 — 31.2%. The text rounds the 2011 figure to 31.16%.

Trend and interpretation

The graph shows a gradual overall rise in India's urbanisation between 1961 and 2011. The smallest increase between consecutive points shown is from 1961 to 1971; the largest increase among these intervals is from 1971 to 1981.

Regional differences described in the chapter

The textbook says urbanisation was higher in southern India than in northern India. It identifies Goa as the most urbanised state in its reference period, at 62%, and says Delhi was more than 80% urban. It lists Tamil Nadu, Maharashtra, Gujarat and Kerala among states with higher urbanisation, and Himachal Pradesh, Jammu and Kashmir, Uttarakhand, Bihar and Rajasthan among those with lower levels.

Historical context

The chapter mentions long-standing settlements such as Indraprastha (Delhi), Mithila, Varanasi, Harappa, Mohenjo-daro, Ujjain and Pratishthan (Paithan) as evidence of India's long urban history.

7. Urbanisation in Brazil

Data from the textbook table

Percentage of urban population: 1960 — 47.1%; 1970 — 56.8%; 1980 — 66.0%; 1990 — 74.6%; 2000 — 81.5%; 2010 — 84.6%.

Interval

The data are given at ten-year intervals.

Trend

Brazil's urban population share rose in every decade shown. The increase was greatest between 1960 and 1970 (9.7 percentage points) and smallest between 2000 and 2010 (3.1 percentage points).

Regional pattern

The chapter says rapid urbanisation occurred mainly in southern and south-eastern Brazil, with São Paulo developing as a major metropolitan and industrial area. Figure 7.6 shows state-wise urban population categories and illustrates greater urbanisation in many coastal and south-eastern states than in much of the interior and north.

Go West policy

The textbook says the government promoted a “Go West” policy to reduce pressure on areas where population was concentrated and reduce regional imbalance. This is described as textbook context; the chapter does not provide a detailed evaluation of the policy.

8. Compare Urbanisation in India and Brazil

Overall comparison

The textbook presents India as less urbanised, with a gradual increase from 18.0% in 1961 to 31.2% in 2011. Brazil's urban share rose from 47.1% in 1960 to 84.6% in 2010, making it much more urbanised in the periods shown.

Location

In India, urbanisation varies by state and the chapter describes higher levels in southern states and in Delhi. In Brazil, urbanisation is particularly prominent in the south and south-east, including São Paulo, and in many coastal states.

Settlement patterns

India has both nucleated settlements in cultivated plains and valleys and dispersed settlements in tribal, hilly and uneven regions. Brazil has dense coastal and south-eastern settlements and sparse settlements in the Amazon Basin and some interior regions.

Density and factors

Both countries have uneven settlement patterns. Water, landforms, climate, resources, transport and economic opportunities help explain why some areas have denser settlements than others.

10. Textbook Exercise — Geographical Reasons

(a) Availability of water is a major factor affecting settlements.

Water is needed for drinking, household activities and agriculture. The textbook identifies water availability as one of the factors influencing settlement patterns. It can support more permanent settlement where other conditions are suitable.

(b) In Brazil, the majority of the population is found in eastern coastal areas.

The textbook says early settlements were formed mainly along the coast. It also describes the coastal region as having water supply and natural resources, while São Paulo benefits from suitable soil, minerals, energy and transport.

(c) Urbanisation is increasing in India.

Figure 7.5 shows India's urban population share increasing from 18.0% in 1961 to 31.2% in 2011. The chapter attributes growth partly to expansion of urban centres and the emergence of new towns.

(d) Settlements are sparse in north-eastern Brazil.

The textbook says rural areas in the north-east highlands suffer from extreme droughts, making agriculture difficult.

(e) Except Delhi and Chandigarh, urbanisation is low in other parts of India.

This statement is too broad to treat as a precise fact. The chapter says urbanisation varies by state, names Goa as highly urbanised in its reference period, and lists several states with relatively higher urbanisation. For an exam answer, refer to the textbook's regional descriptions and data rather than repeating the statement without qualification.

11. Textbook Exercise — Short Answers

(a) Write a comparative note on urbanisation in Brazil and India.

According to the textbook data, India's urban population share rose from 18.0% in 1961 to 31.2% in 2011. Brazil's rose from 47.1% in 1960 to 84.6% in 2010. Thus, Brazil was much more urbanised in the periods compared. In India, the chapter highlights higher urbanisation in the south and in Delhi; in Brazil, it highlights the south and south-east, especially São Paulo.

(b) Differentiate between human settlements in the Ganga and Amazon river basins.

The textbook links dense settlement in India's cultivated plains with fertile land and access to water. In the Amazon Basin, settlements are sparse because of thick equatorial rainforest, difficult accessibility, climate conditions and poor transport links.

(c) Why do human settlements grow only in specific locations?

The textbook identifies climate, water availability, slope and shape of land and level of development as important factors. Natural resources, cultivable soil, transport and economic activity also influence the development and concentration of settlements.
