Complete Solutions / Study Notes

Rumours • Miscommunication • Panic • Verification of Facts

1. Chapter Overview

“The Boy Who Broke the Bank” by Ruskin Bond is a humorous story about the rapid spread of a rumour. Nathu, a sweeper-boy at the Pipalnagar Bank, is unhappy because his salary has not been paid. His ordinary complaint is passed from one person to another and gradually changes into the false news that the bank is about to collapse.

The rumour spreads through the bazaar, creates fear among depositors and finally causes a crowd to gather outside the bank. The story shows how misunderstood information can become exaggerated and how people may react without checking whether a claim is true.

Central message: Information should be checked before it is believed or passed on. Rumours can spread quickly and may cause unnecessary fear, confusion and damage.

2. Author — Ruskin Bond

The textbook introduces Ruskin Bond (1934) as an Indian author of British descent who lives with his adopted family in Landour, Mussoorie, India. It also states that the Indian Council for Child Education recognised his role in the growth of children's literature in India. The textbook notes his Sahitya Akademi Award in 1992 for Our Trees Still Grow in Dehra, and Padma Shri and Padma Bhushan awards in 1999 and 2014 respectively.

3. Warming Up — Rumour and Fact

A. Chit-Chat

“A lie can run round the world before truth has got its boots on.” This quotation expresses how quickly rumours can spread.

How can we decide whether news is fact or fake?

  • Check the original or official source.
  • Compare the information with more than one reliable source.
  • Check the date, context and evidence.
  • Do not forward sensational messages merely because they sound convincing.
  • Separate confirmed facts from opinions, guesses and anonymous claims.

Who spreads false news? False news may be spread by people who misunderstand information, repeat unverified claims, deliberately mislead others or forward messages without checking them.

Do you believe all news? No. News should be checked before being accepted or shared.

4. Story in Detail

Beginning — Nathu's Complaint

Nathu is sweeping the steps of the Pipalnagar Bank, owned by Seth Govind Ram. He is annoyed because he has not received his regular salary even though it is already the twentieth of the month. He tells Sitaram, the washerman's son, that he wants to leave the job once he gets his money.

Sitaram Helps Nathu

Sitaram says he will look for a suitable job for Nathu. At a house he visits, he hears Mrs. Srivastava mention that she needs a sweeper. Sitaram tells her about Nathu and she asks him to send Nathu to see her the next day.

The First Transformation of the Information

Mrs. Srivastava later meets Mrs. Bhushan at the market. She tells her that the Pipalnagar Bank cannot even pay its employees and that its sweeper has not received wages for over a month. Mrs. Bhushan immediately assumes that if the sweeper is unpaid, the other employees may also be unpaid.

The Rumour Becomes “Bankruptcy”

Mrs. Bhushan tells her husband that the bank is about to go bankrupt. Mr. Bhushan tells photographer Kamal Kishore. Kamal Kishore then warns Deep Chand, the barber, to withdraw his money because the bank is supposedly about to collapse.

Fear Spreads Through the Bazaar

Deep Chand is so startled that he accidentally cuts his customer's ear. The elderly customer hears that Seth Govind Ram has left town for Kashmir and interprets this as further evidence that the bank is collapsing. He rushes away with his cheque book. The news spreads through the bazaar “with the rapidity of forest fire.”

Ganpat's Unexpected Reaction

Old Ganpat, a beggar with a crooked leg, normally does not walk. When he hears that the bank may collapse, he suddenly runs towards the bank because he has a thousand rupees in savings. His reaction surprises everyone.

The Four Rumours about Seth Govind Ram

  1. He had fled the State.
  2. He had fled the country.
  3. He was hiding in Pipalnagar.
  4. He had hanged himself from the tamarind tree and had been found by Nathu.

The Bank Panic

By noon the bank has used all its ready cash. The manager is in a dilemma: emergency funds can only be obtained from another bank some thirty miles away, and he cannot contact Seth Govind Ram, who is on his houseboat in Kashmir. Customers are told to return the following day, but the crowd becomes angry.

People demand their money immediately. Mischief makers join the crowd and make the mood worse. The manager tries to placate them and explains that the bank has money but no immediate means of collecting it. The crowd continues shouting, and finally a brick is thrown through the bank's plate glass window.

Ending — Nathu Learns the News

The next morning Nathu arrives to sweep the bank steps. He sees refuse, stones and broken glass and is angry that his work has increased. Sitaram arrives and tells him that the bank is going out of business. Nathu is surprised and wonders how the bank could have collapsed. He does not realise that his original complaint became the starting point of the rumour.

10(a). Expand the Theme — “Rumours are spread by fools and accepted by greater fools.”

Think Before You Believe

Rumours usually begin with information that has not been properly checked. A small fact may be misunderstood and then repeated to another person. As it passes from one person to another, people may add their own assumptions. Soon the original information can become completely different.

In The Boy Who Broke the Bank, Nathu only complains that his salary has not been paid. The information changes into a claim that the bank cannot pay its employees, then into a rumour that the bank is bankrupt, and finally into a panic among depositors. People rush to withdraw their savings without first verifying the information. The result is anger, fear and damage to the bank property.

The story therefore teaches us to pause, check reliable sources and separate facts from rumours before believing or forwarding information.

10(b). Expand the Theme — “Books are our real friends.”

Books — Our Real Companions

Books are valuable companions because they are always available to teach and guide us. They open a world of knowledge and introduce us to different people, places, ideas and experiences. Reading improves language skills, vocabulary and confidence. Good books can keep us focused and encourage positive habits. They can also contribute to personality development by helping us understand different situations and viewpoints.

Books are useful when we are alone because they give us meaningful company. They can inspire us to think, learn and communicate better. Regular reading therefore enriches both our knowledge and our imagination. For these reasons, books can become dependable friends throughout life.

D. Subject–Verb Agreement

In the simple present tense, the verb generally takes -s/-es when the subject is he, she or it.
  • I want a pen.
  • They want a pen.
  • He wants a pen.
  • She goes home in time.
Auxiliary verbs must also agree with the subject:
  • I am writing an essay.
  • We are writing an essay.
  • He/She is writing an essay.
  • I have written an essay.
  • He/She has written an essay.
  • I was writing an essay.
  • We were writing an essay.

11. Model Long Answers

Q. Explain the title “The Boy Who Broke the Bank”.

The title refers to Nathu, the sweeper-boy, although he does not literally break the bank. Nathu is simply angry because his salary has been delayed. He complains to Sitaram that he wants to leave his job. This information passes through several people and becomes increasingly exaggerated. Soon people believe that the Pipalnagar Bank is bankrupt and rush to withdraw their money. The manager cannot provide the money immediately, the crowd becomes violent and a brick breaks the bank's window. Nathu remains unaware of the chain of events until the following morning. The title is therefore humorous because Nathu's small complaint indirectly becomes the starting point of the crisis.

Q. Explain how the rumour spread.

The rumour begins with Nathu's complaint that his salary has not been paid. Sitaram knows about it and tells Mrs. Srivastava while discussing a possible job for Nathu. Mrs. Srivastava tells Mrs. Bhushan that the bank cannot pay its employees. Mrs. Bhushan interprets this as a sign that the bank is in a bad financial condition. The information reaches Mr. Bhushan, then Kamal Kishore, and then Deep Chand. It changes from “the sweeper has not received his wages” into “the bank is about to collapse.” The news then spreads through the bazaar, creating a panic among depositors.

Q. What lesson does the story teach?

The story teaches us to verify information before believing or spreading it. A rumour may begin with a small fact but can become exaggerated as it passes from one person to another. In the story, an unpaid salary becomes a false report of bank bankruptcy and finally causes panic and damage. Responsible communication requires checking reliable information and distinguishing facts from assumptions.

Q. Why was the manager unable to satisfy the crowd?

The bank had gone through its ready cash because many customers demanded their money at the same time. Emergency funds could be obtained only from another bank about thirty miles away. The manager could not contact Seth Govind Ram, who was in Kashmir, and he was not sure the crowd would wait. He therefore asked people to return the next day, but they became angry and the situation escalated.
