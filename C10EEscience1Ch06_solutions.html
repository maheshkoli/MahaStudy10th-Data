1. Chapter at a Glance

Light usually travels in straight lines, but its direction changes when it passes from one transparent medium to another. This change explains the apparent bending of a pencil in water, the visibility of a coin under water, mirages, the twinkling of stars and the formation of rainbows.

Refraction

Change in direction of light as it passes between transparent media.

Refractive index

Related to the speed of light in a medium.

Dispersion

Separation of white light into its component colours.

Total internal reflection

Light is reflected completely back into the denser medium under specified conditions.

2. Refraction of Light

Refraction of light is the change in direction of light when it passes from one transparent medium to another, such as from air into water or glass. Refraction occurs because the speed of light differs in different media.

Activities from the textbook

  • Pencil in water: A pencil partly immersed in water appears thicker and seems bent or broken near the water surface.
  • Coin in a vessel: A coin hidden by the rim of a vessel becomes visible when water is added. Light from the coin changes direction at the water–air boundary and reaches the observer.
  • Glass slab: A ray bends on entering the slab and bends again on leaving it. For parallel faces, the emergent ray is parallel to the incident ray but is laterally displaced.
Glass slab diagram — textbook page 74: The first refraction occurs at the air-to-glass surface and the second at the glass-to-air surface. The two deviations are in opposite directions, so the emergent ray is parallel to the incident ray.

3. Laws of Refraction

  1. The incident ray, the refracted ray and the normal at the point of incidence lie in the same plane. The incident and refracted rays lie on opposite sides of the normal.
  2. For a given pair of media, the ratio of the sine of the angle of incidence to the sine of the angle of refraction is constant. This is the second law of refraction, also called Snell’s law.
sin i / sin r = constant = n

Here, i is the angle of incidence, r is the angle of refraction, and n represents the refractive index of the second medium with respect to the first, using the textbook’s notation.

If a ray is incident along the normal, then i = 0° and r = 0°; it continues in the same direction.

4. Refractive Index

The refractive index describes how light propagates in a medium. It depends on the velocity of light in that medium and, as the chapter explains, differs for different media and different colours of light.

Relative refractive index

If the speed of light in medium 1 is v₁ and in medium 2 is v₂, the refractive index of medium 2 with respect to medium 1 is:

Refractive index of medium 2 with respect to medium 1 = v₁ / v₂

The refractive index of medium 1 with respect to medium 2 is v₂ / v₁. These two relative refractive indices are reciprocals.

Absolute refractive index

When the first medium is vacuum, the refractive index of the second medium is called its absolute refractive index.

n = c / v

Here, c is the speed of light in vacuum (approximately 3 × 10⁸ m/s) and v is the speed of light in the medium.

Light bends towards the normal.

When light is incident normally at the boundary, its direction does not change.

5. Atmospheric Refraction

Air temperature and density vary from place to place and with height. These changes affect the refractive index of air, causing light rays to follow curved paths in the atmosphere.

Mirage

Near a hot road or desert surface, air is hotter and rarer than the air above it. Light from a distant object bends as it travels through air layers with different refractive indices. The light appears to come from an image below the ground, creating the illusion of water. This is called a mirage (textbook Figure 6.5, page 76).

Twinkling of stars

Stars are very far away and appear as point sources. As starlight passes through atmospheric layers of changing density and refractive index, its apparent position and brightness change continuously. This is why stars appear to twinkle.

Why planets do not noticeably twinkle

The textbook explains that planets are closer and appear as collections of point sources rather than single point sources. Changes affecting individual points average out, so their overall position and brightness remain nearly unchanged.

Early sunrise and delayed sunset

Atmospheric refraction bends sunlight along a curved path, allowing us to see the Sun when it is still slightly below the horizon. For the same reason, the Sun remains visible for a short time after it has gone below the horizon.

6. Dispersion of Light

Dispersion of light is the separation of white light into its component colours when it passes through a medium. A glass prism can separate sunlight into a spectrum of seven colours: violet, indigo, blue, green, yellow, orange and red (VIBGYOR).

  • Visible wavelengths are approximately 400–700 nm, according to the textbook.
  • Red light has the longest wavelength (close to 700 nm) and bends the least in the prism.
  • Violet light has the shortest wavelength (close to 400 nm) and bends the most.
  • Different colours travel at different speeds in a medium, so they have different refractive indices and refract by different amounts.
Rainbow: Water droplets act like tiny prisms. Sunlight is refracted and dispersed as it enters a droplet, reflected internally, and refracted again as it leaves. The combined effects produce a rainbow (Figure 6.10, page 78).

Textbook “Use your brain power” prompts

How can two prisms produce white emergent light? Place a second prism in the opposite orientation to the first so that the separated colours are recombined.

Will an LED produce the same spectrum as a tungsten bulb through a glass prism? The observed colours depend on the light source’s spectrum. A white LED may produce a spectrum, but it may not be continuous or identical to the spectrum of a tungsten bulb.

7. Partial Reflection and Total Internal Reflection

When light travels from a denser medium to a rarer medium, some light may be reflected back into the denser medium while the rest is refracted into the rarer medium. This is called partial reflection.

Critical angle

As the angle of incidence in the denser medium increases, the angle of refraction also increases. The angle of incidence for which the angle of refraction becomes 90° is called the critical angle.

Total internal reflection

When light travels from a denser to a rarer medium and its angle of incidence is greater than the critical angle, the light is reflected completely back into the denser medium. This phenomenon is called total internal reflection.

For a denser medium to air: n = sin i / sin 90° = sin ic

Here, ic is the critical angle, and sin 90° = 1. This expression follows the textbook’s derivation for the denser medium relative to air.

8. Solved Examples

Example 1 — Speed of light in water

Given: Absolute refractive index n = 1.36; speed of light in vacuum c = 3 × 10⁸ m/s. The value n = 1.36 is the value used in the textbook’s solved example.

Formula: n = c/v, so v = c/n.

v = (3 × 10⁸) / 1.36 ≈ 2.21 × 10⁸ m/s

Answer: 2.21 × 10⁸ m/s.

Example 2 — Relative refractive index

Given: Speed in medium 1, v₁ = 1.5 × 10⁸ m/s; speed in medium 2, v₂ = 0.75 × 10⁸ m/s.

Refractive index of medium 2 with respect to medium 1 = v₁/v₂
n = (1.5 × 10⁸) / (0.75 × 10⁸) = 2

Answer: 2.

9. Textbook Exercise Solutions

Q1. Fill in the blanks and explain the completed sentences.

a. Refractive index depends on the ______ of light.
Answer: velocity (speed).
Explanation: The refractive index is related to the speed of light in the medium.

b. The change in ______ of light rays while going from one medium to another is called refraction.
Answer: direction.
Explanation: Light changes direction when it passes between media in which its speed differs.

Q2(a). Prove that the angle of incidence and the angle of emergence for a ray passing through a glass slab are equal.

Let the angle of incidence be i, the angle inside the glass be r, and the angle of emergence be e. For parallel faces, the internal angle at the second face is also r.

At first surface: sin i / sin r = nglass / nair
At second surface: sin r / sin e = nair / nglass

Multiplying the equations cancels the refractive indices and sin r:

sin i / sin e = 1 ⇒ sin i = sin e ⇒ i = e

Hence proved: the angle of incidence equals the angle of emergence. The emergent ray is parallel to the incident ray, though laterally displaced.

Q2(b). Explain why a rainbow is the combined effect of refraction, dispersion and total internal reflection.

Answer: Sunlight is refracted and dispersed when it enters a water droplet. It is then reflected internally and refracted again as it exits. These processes separate the sunlight into colours and produce a rainbow.

Q3(A). What is the reason for the twinkling of stars?

Correct option: iv. Changing refractive index of the atmospheric gases.

Q3(B). Why can we see the Sun when it is a little below the horizon?

Correct option: ii. Refraction of light.

Q3(C). If the refractive index of glass with respect to air is 3/2, what is the refractive index of air with respect to glass?

nair w.r.t. glass = 1 ÷ (3/2) = 2/3

Correct option: 2/3 (option c in the printed question).

Q4(a). If the speed of light in a medium is 1.5 × 10⁸ m/s, find its absolute refractive index.

n = c/v = (3 × 10⁸) / (1.5 × 10⁸) = 2

Answer: 2.

Q4(b). Absolute refractive indices of glass and water are 3/2 and 4/3. Find the refractive index of glass with respect to water.

nglass w.r.t. water = nglass / nwater
= (3/2) ÷ (4/3) = (3/2) × (3/4) = 9/8

Answer: 9/8.

10. Quick Revision Sheet

  • Refraction: Change in direction of light when it enters another transparent medium.
  • Snell’s law: sin i / sin r = constant for a given pair of media.
  • Absolute refractive index: n = c/v.
  • Relative refractive index: speed in first medium ÷ speed in second medium.
  • Rarer to denser: bends towards the normal.
  • Denser to rarer: bends away from the normal.
  • Mirage: Refraction through air layers of different temperatures and refractive indices.
  • Twinkling of stars: Changing atmospheric refraction.
  • Dispersion: White light separates into VIBGYOR; red bends least and violet most in a prism.
  • Critical angle: Angle of incidence when angle of refraction is 90°.
  • Total internal reflection: Light is reflected completely into the denser medium when incidence exceeds the critical angle, for light travelling from denser to rarer medium.
Exam tip: For relative refractive index, write down which medium the light starts in and which it enters. The speed in the first medium goes in the numerator.
