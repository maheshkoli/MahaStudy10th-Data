Meaning and Features of a Movement

A movement is a form of collective action in which people actively participate to pursue a particular issue or social objective. A movement focuses on a specific problem, organises people around it, builds public opinion and tries to influence the government or political parties.

Main features

  1. Collective action: People come together and participate actively.
  2. A focused issue or objective: For example, a movement against corruption focuses on ending corruption, while pollution can become the focus of an environmental movement.
  3. Leadership: Leaders keep a movement active and decide its objectives, programme of action and strategy.
  4. Organisation: An organisation helps members coordinate activities and follow up on the issue.
  5. Public support: People need to feel connected to the issue. A programme of action helps build public opinion.

The textbook notes that movements often focus on one issue but may share wider values such as democracy, women’s empowerment and social equality. Some movements may gradually transform into political parties.

Why Are Movements Important in a Democracy?

Political parties usually consider many issues across society, while movements can focus attention on one particular problem. They organise people, keep an issue in public discussion and put pressure on political parties and the government to respond.

  • They bring social problems into public discussion.
  • They provide information to the government that can help in policy-making.
  • They help citizens participate in public life beyond elections.
  • They can oppose a government decision or policy.
  • They help shape public opinion and encourage accountability.

The textbook describes the right to protest as an important democratic right, which should be exercised patiently and responsibly. It also notes that movements existed in Britain during the democratic system’s development and treats the existence of movements as an indicator of a healthy democracy.

Important Movements in India

1. Tribal movement

The textbook explains that during the pre-independence period, British policies deprived tribal communities of livelihood connected with forest resources. It mentions uprisings involving Kolam, Gond, Koli, Bhilla, Ramoshi, Santhal and Munda communities, and highlights Birsa Munda. A major demand described in the chapter is recognition of tribal rights over forests, including collecting forest produce and cultivating forest land.

2. Farmers’ movement

Farmers began organising in response to anti-agricultural policies under British rule. The chapter refers to Bardoli, Champaranya and movements against revenue collection, inspired by Mahatma Phule, Justice Ranade and Mahatma Gandhi. It says reforms in tenancy and land laws slowed the movement for a time, while the Green Revolution was followed by renewed activity because its benefits did not reach all farmers equally.

Demands mentioned: fair prices for agricultural produce, treating agriculture as an industry, implementing recommendations of the Swaminathan Commission, debt relief or cancellation, and a national agricultural policy. Organisations named include Shetkari Sanghatana, Bharatiya Kisan Union and All India Kisan Sabha.

3. Trade union / workers’ movement

The textbook links trade unions to industrialisation, including textile mills and railways. Railway workers went on strike in 1899, and the All India Trade Union Congress was established in 1920 as an organisation to address workers’ issues. The chapter says trade unions became more effective after independence, organised several agitations in the 1960s and 1970s, and became more fragmented from the 1980s, with globalisation affecting them.

Worker problems listed: unstable employment, contract labour, financial insecurity, lack of legal protection, unlimited working hours, workplace insecurity and health hazards.

4. Women’s movement

In the pre-independence period, reformers worked against injustice and exploitation of women and promoted dignity and participation in social life. The textbook names Ishwarchandra Vidyasagar, Raja Rammohan Roy, Mahatma Jyotirao Phule, Savitribai Phule, Maharshi Dhondo Keshav Karve, Pandita Ramabai and Ramabai Ranade. Their work supported reforms such as widow remarriage, women’s education and women’s right to vote, and helped oppose sati and child marriage.

After independence, the Constitution granted equal rights, but inequality persisted. The women’s movement raised issues including health, social security, financial independence, empowerment and equal education. The chapter notes women’s participation in movements against corruption, caste discrimination and religious extremism.

5. Environmental movements

Environmental movements address environmental degradation and issues such as biodiversity, water sources, forests, green belts, river pollution and harmful effects of chemicals. The textbook highlights Dr Rajendrasinh Rana, also known as the “Waterman of India,” for work on johads, river revival and water conservation in Rajasthan through Tarun Bharat Sangh. The book says his work included building thousands of johads and conserving forests and wildlife.

6. Consumer movement

The textbook says the consumer movement in India emerged after the Consumer Protection Act came into existence in 1986. It aims to protect consumers from problems such as adulteration, increased prices, fraud in weights and measures, and other forms of cheating.

New Social Movements

The textbook calls movements that emerged after the 1980s neo-social movements. It describes them as issue-based mass movements that differ in nature from earlier movements. Their work can focus on a particular issue and bring together people around a shared concern.

The chapter’s opening examples include movements against child marriage and dowry, along with the need for campaigns addressing malnutrition. It also asks students to collect news about movements such as Andhashraddha Nirmulan, river-pollution campaigns, movements against female foeticide and “Not in My Name.” These are examples for classroom investigation rather than a complete list of all movements.

3. Answer the following questions briefly

(1) Explain the activities/role/functions of the environmental movement.
Answer: Environmental movements work to protect the environment and address environmental degradation. The textbook lists biodiversity protection, conservation of water sources and forests, development of green belts, prevention of river pollution, and reducing the harmful effects of chemicals. It also gives the example of water conservation and river revival work in Rajasthan.

(2) Explain the nature of the farmers’ movement in India.
Answer: Farmers organised during British rule in response to policies harmful to agriculture. The textbook refers to Bardoli, Champaranya and revenue-related struggles, influenced by Mahatma Phule, Justice Ranade and Mahatma Gandhi. Later, farmers’ movements became active again after the Green Revolution did not benefit all farmers equally. Their demands include fair prices, debt relief, implementation of the Swaminathan Commission’s recommendations and a national agricultural policy.

(3) What reforms did the women’s movement fight for in the pre-independence period?
Answer: Reformers worked against sati and child marriage and supported widow remarriage, women’s education and women’s right to vote. 4. Explain the statements with reasons

(1) Movements are important in a democracy.
Answer: Movements bring public attention to social problems, organise people around specific issues and put pressure on the government and political parties to act. They provide information useful for policy-making, shape public opinion and allow citizens to participate in public life. The textbook considers the right to protest an important democratic right.

(2) Movements do not need strong leadership.
Answer: False. The textbook explains that leadership keeps a movement active. Leaders decide its objectives, programme of action and strategy. Strong leadership can make a movement more effective, so leadership is an important feature of a movement.

(3) Consumer movement came into existence.
Answer: The consumer movement emerged in India after the Consumer Protection Act came into existence in 1986. It seeks to protect consumers from adulteration, increased prices, fraud in weights and measures, and other unfair practices.

Important Questions and Quick Revision

Important questions

What is a movement?
A movement is collective action in which people actively participate to pursue a focused issue or social objective.

What makes a movement effective?
Focused objectives, active public participation, leadership, organisation, a programme of action and public support help a movement remain active and influence decision-makers.

What are the main demands of farmers’ movements described in the chapter?
Fair prices for agricultural products, treating agriculture as an industry, implementation of the Swaminathan Commission recommendations, debt relief or cancellation and a national agricultural policy.

What issues do environmental movements address?
Protection of biodiversity, water sources and forests; green belts; river pollution; and the harmful effects of chemicals.

Quick revision sheet

  • Movement: Collective action focused on an issue.
  • Core features: Issue, objective, leadership, organisation, public support and programme of action.
  • Tribal movement: Forest rights and livelihood.
  • Farmers’ movement: Fair prices, debt relief and agricultural policy.
  • Workers’ movement: Employment conditions and worker protections.
  • Women’s movement: Ending injustice and promoting equal rights and empowerment.
  • Environmental movement: Protecting nature and reducing environmental degradation.
  • Consumer movement: Protecting consumers from unfair practices.
  • 1986: Consumer Protection Act, as cited in the textbook.
  • After the 1980s: The textbook refers to neo-social movements as issue-based mass movements.
