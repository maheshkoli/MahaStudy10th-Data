1. Chapter at a Glance

This chapter explores the history and technology of space exploration. It covers the classification of artificial satellites, their orbits, and their various functions. It also discusses satellite launch vehicles, the physics behind space missions (such as escape velocity), and India's significant contributions to space research.

2. Important Concepts Explained

Space Missions

Space missions are categorized into two types:

  1. Earth-orbiting missions: The objective is to place artificial satellites in orbit around the Earth for research and various applications.
  2. Deep space missions: The objective is to send spacecraft to outer space for close observation of objects in the solar system or beyond.

Artificial Satellites

An artificial satellite is a man-made object that revolves around the Earth or any other planet in a fixed orbit.

  • Power Source: Satellites work on solar energy, using solar photovoltaic panels attached like wings.
  • Functionality: They are equipped with instruments to receive and transmit signals to and from Earth.

Classification of Satellites

Satellites are classified based on their functions:

  • Weather satellite: Study and prediction of weather.
  • Communication satellite: Establish communication between different locations on Earth.
  • Broadcast satellite: Telecasting of television programs.
  • Navigational satellite: Fix the location of any place on Earth's surface with precise latitude and longitude.
  • Military Satellite: Collect information for security aspects.
  • Earth Observation Satellite: Study forests, deserts, oceans, polar ice, and manage natural resources/disasters.

Orbits of Artificial Satellites

Satellites are placed in specific orbits based on their function:

  • High Earth Orbits (HEO): Height > 35,780 km. These include geosynchronous satellites, which appear stationary relative to Earth. They are used for meteorology and communication.
  • Medium Earth Orbits (MEO): Height between 2,000 km and 35,780 km. Used for polar orbits and global positioning systems.
  • Low Earth Orbits (LEO): Height between 180 km and 2,000 km. Used for scientific experiments and atmospheric studies (e.g., International Space Station, Hubble telescope).

Satellite Launch Vehicles

Launch vehicles are based on Newton's third law of motion. Fuel combustion produces high-temperature gas that is expelled forcefully through nozzles, creating a thrust that drives the vehicle into space. To reduce weight during flight, multi-stage launch vehicles are used.

Space Missions Away from Earth

To travel into outer space, a spacecraft must escape Earth's gravitational force. This requires an initial velocity greater than the escape velocity.

3. Definitions and Key Terms

  • Artificial Satellite: A man-made object revolving around a planet in a fixed orbit.
  • Geosynchronous Satellite: A satellite in a high Earth orbit that appears stationary relative to a point on Earth.
  • Escape Velocity: The minimum velocity required for an object to escape the gravitational influence of a planet.
  • Space Debris: Non-functional satellites, detached parts of launchers, and debris from collisions revolving around the Earth.

4. Formulas, Rules, and Properties

  • Critical Velocity ($v_c$): The velocity required to maintain a stable orbit at height 'h'.

$$v_c = \sqrt{\frac{GM}{R+h}}$$

Where:

  • $G = 6.67 \times 10^{-11} \text{ N m}^2/\text{kg}^2$
  • $M = 6 \times 10^{24} \text{ kg}$ (Mass of Earth)
  • $R = 6.4 \times 10^6 \text{ m}$ (Radius of Earth)
  • Escape Velocity ($v_{esc}$):

$$v_{esc} = \sqrt{\frac{2GM}{R}}$$

For Earth, this value is approximately $11.2 \text{ km/s}$.

5. Worked Examples — Step by Step

Example 1: Tangential velocity of a satellite

Given:

  • Height ($h$) = $35,780 \text{ km} = 35,780 \times 10^3 \text{ m}$
  • $R = 6,400 \text{ km} = 6.4 \times 10^6 \text{ m}$
  • $M = 6 \times 10^{24} \text{ kg}$
  • $G = 6.67 \times 10^{-11} \text{ N m}^2/\text{kg}^2$

Calculation:

  1. $R + h = 6,400 + 35,780 = 42,180 \text{ km} = 42,180 \times 10^3 \text{ m}$
  2. $v = \sqrt{\frac{GM}{R+h}}$
  3. $v = \sqrt{\frac{(6.67 \times 10^{-11}) \times (6 \times 10^{24})}{42,180 \times 10^3}}$
  4. $v = \sqrt{\frac{40.02 \times 10^{13}}{42,180 \times 10^3}} = \sqrt{0.00094879 \times 10^{10}} = \sqrt{9487909}$
  5. $v \approx 3080.245 \text{ m/s} = 3.08 \text{ km/s}$

Example 2: Time for one revolution

Given:

  • Height ($h$) = $35,780 \text{ km}$
  • Velocity ($v$) = $3.08 \text{ km/s}$

Calculation:

  1. Radius of orbit ($r$) = $R + h = 6,400 + 35,780 = 42,180 \text{ km}$
  2. Circumference = $2\pi r = 2 \times 3.14 \times 42,180 \text{ km}$
  3. Time ($T$) = $\frac{\text{Circumference}}{v} = \frac{2 \times 3.14 \times 42,180}{3.08}$
  4. $T \approx 86,003.38 \text{ seconds} \approx 23.89 \text{ hours} \approx 23 \text{ hours } 54 \text{ minutes}$

6. Exercise / Practice Set Solutions

1. Fill in the blanks and explain with reasoning:

(a) If the height of the orbit of a satellite from the earth surface is increased, the tangential velocity of the satellite will decrease.

Reasoning: The formula for critical velocity is $v_c = \sqrt{\frac{GM}{R+h}}$. As height ($h$) increases, the denominator increases, causing the velocity ($v_c$) to decrease.

(b) The initial velocity (during launching) of the Mangalyaan must be greater than escape velocity of the earth.

Reasoning: To leave Earth's gravitational influence and travel to Mars, the spacecraft must reach a velocity at least equal to the escape velocity ($11.2 \text{ km/s}$).

2. State with reasons whether the following sentences are true or false:

(a) If a spacecraft has to be sent away from the influence of earth’s gravitational field, its velocity must be less than the escape velocity.

False. It must be equal to or greater than the escape velocity.

(b) The escape velocity on the moon is less than that on the earth.

True. The escape velocity depends on the mass and radius of the planet/satellite. Since the Moon's mass is much smaller than Earth's, its escape velocity is lower.

(d) A satellite needs a specific velocity to revolve in a specific orbit.

True. This is the critical velocity required to balance the gravitational pull with the centripetal force at that specific height.

(e) If the height of the orbit of a satellite increases, its velocity must also increase.

False. As shown by the formula $v_c = \sqrt{\frac{GM}{R+h}}$, increasing height ($h$) decreases the required critical velocity.

3. Answer the following questions:

(a) Artificial satellite: A man-made object revolving around a planet. They are classified by function: Weather, Communication, Broadcast, Navigational, Military, and Earth Observation.

(b) Orbit: The path followed by a satellite around a planet. Orbits are classified by height: High Earth Orbit (HEO), Medium Earth Orbit (MEO), and Low Earth Orbit (LEO).

(c) Geostationary satellites: These orbit above the equator. They are not useful for polar regions because they cannot "see" the poles due to the Earth's curvature.

(d) Satellite launch vehicles: Vehicles used to place satellites into orbit. ISRO's PSLV (Polar Satellite Launch Vehicle) uses multiple stages to shed weight and increase speed.

(e) Multi-stage launch vehicles: It is beneficial because it reduces the vehicle's weight step-by-step as fuel is exhausted, allowing the remaining stages to achieve higher speeds with less fuel.

5. Solve the following problems:

(a) If mass of a planet is 8 times Earth's mass ($M_p = 8M_e$) and radius is twice Earth's radius ($R_p = 2R_e$):

  • $v_{esc} = \sqrt{\frac{2GM}{R}}$
  • $v_{esc(p)} = \sqrt{\frac{2G(8M_e)}{2R_e}} = \sqrt{4 \times \frac{2GM_e}{R_e}} = 2 \times v_{esc(e)}$
  • $2 \times 11.2 \text{ km/s} = \mathbf{22.4 \text{ km/s}}$

(b) Time for satellite at $35,780 \text{ km}$ if Earth's mass were 4 times original:

  • $v = \sqrt{\frac{G(4M)}{r}} = 2 \times v_{original}$
  • Since $T = \frac{2\pi r}{v}$, if $v$ doubles, $T$ becomes half.
  • Original $T \approx 24 \text{ hours}$, so new $T \approx \mathbf{12 \text{ hours}}$.

(c) Height of satellite for $2\sqrt{2}T$ seconds:

  • Using Kepler's Third Law: $T^2 \propto r^3$ (where $r = R+h$)
  • $(2\sqrt{2}T)^2 \propto r_{new}^3 \implies 8T^2 \propto r_{new}^3$
  • Since $T^2 \propto (R+h_1)^3$, then $8(R+h_1)^3 \propto r_{new}^3$
  • $r_{new} = \sqrt[3]{8} \times (R+h_1) = 2(R+h_1) = 2R + 2h_1$
  • Height $h_{new} = r_{new} - R = 2R + 2h_1 - R = \mathbf{R + 2h_1}$

7. Important Questions and Answers

  • Q: Why do satellites need solar panels?

A: Satellites operate in space where there is no power grid; solar panels convert sunlight into electricity to power their instruments.

  • Q: What is space debris and why is it a problem?

A: Space debris consists of non-functional satellites and fragments from collisions. It is dangerous because it can collide with active satellites and spacecraft, causing damage.

8. Common Errors and How to Avoid Them

  • Confusing Orbit Height and Orbital Radius: Remember that $r = R + h$, where $R$ is Earth's radius and $h$ is the height above the surface. Always add $R$ to $h$ before using $r$ in formulas.
  • Unit Mismatch: Ensure all distances are in meters and masses in kilograms when using the gravitational constant $G$.
