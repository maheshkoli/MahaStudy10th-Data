1. Chapter Overview

The lesson presents Madame Marie Curie as an extraordinary scientist who dedicated her life to science and the welfare of humanity. The text follows her journey from poverty and hunger in Warsaw and Paris to her scientific work with Pierre Curie, the discovery of Polonium and Radium, international recognition, personal loss and her continued commitment to science. fileciteturn191file0L46-L71

The central message is that great achievement requires courage, commitment, dedication, endurance and an indomitable spirit. Marie Curie's life is shown as an example of strength of character even under extremely hostile and unfavourable conditions. fileciteturn191file0L257-L271

2. Warming Up — Model Answers

1. Have you ever participated in or visited a science exhibition?

Yes. I have visited a science exhibition. It was interesting because the models and experiments made scientific ideas easier to understand.

2. Did you observe or learn anything new?

Yes. I learned how scientific principles can be demonstrated through working models. I also understood that experiments require careful observation, patience and accuracy.

3. Why do scientists work hard and make sacrifices?

Scientists often have to repeat experiments, overcome failures, collect evidence and work for long periods before reaching a result. They may also face financial, physical or practical difficulties. Persistence is therefore important for achieving success in scientific research.

6. Marie and Pierre Curie

At university Marie met Pierre Curie, described as a brilliant but poor French scientist. They worked together in a shabby laboratory and married in July 1895. Their home had very little furniture. After the birth of their daughter Irene, they established a laboratory in a wooden shed near their flat. fileciteturn191file0L111-L123

Signs of Poverty

  • Their Paris flat had scarcely any furniture.
  • Their laboratory was a wooden shed with a leaky skylight and earthen floor.
  • They could not afford large quantities of expensive pitchblende.
  • They avoided costly food and warm clothes.
  • They sometimes could not sleep because of the cold.

The conditions are described in the textbook's account of their laboratory and research. fileciteturn191file0L116-L123 fileciteturn191file0L143-L176

7. Discovery of Polonium and Radium

Marie was especially interested in uranium obtained from pitchblende. She discovered that the material left after obtaining uranium was even more powerful. Marie and Pierre later found evidence of two new substances giving off these rays. They named one Polonium, in honour of Marie's country, Poland, and the other Radium. fileciteturn191file0L124-L142

Why were they called Polonium and Radium?

Polonium: It was named in honour of Marie's country, Poland.
Radium: The text gives the name Radium for the other newly identified radioactive substance.

What is pitchblende?

Pitchblende is described in the textbook as a black, very hard and expensive mineral containing radium. fileciteturn191file0L124-L126

What do radioactive rays do?

The text explains that radioactive elements can give off rays that can penetrate substances that are opaque to light. fileciteturn191file0L136-L142

8. Poverty, Determination and the Breakthrough

The Curies sacrificed the luxuries of life to purchase whatever small amount of pitchblende they could afford. They lived in extreme poverty and worked in difficult temperatures. Marie's health was affected by overwork, but she resolutely refused to abandon the research. fileciteturn191file0L170-L184

The Emperor of Austria's Gift

The Emperor of Austria gave the Curies a ton of pitchblende. It was precious because pitchblende was extremely expensive and the Curies could not afford to buy it in large quantities. The gift enabled them to continue their demanding research with a much larger supply of material. fileciteturn191file0L185-L197

First Sight of Radium

After four more years of work, the Curies returned to their laboratory on a September night in 1902. In the dark laboratory they saw soft, bluish-purple rays coming from the small glass-covered bowls containing the processed material. The text identifies this moment as the discovery of radium. The actual quantity produced was only a tiny pinch of white powder resembling salt. fileciteturn191file0L193-L212

9. Benefits and Humanitarian Use of Radium

The text states that radium had important medical uses. It says that radium was used effectively in the treatment of cancer and that the bacteria of diseases such as typhus, cholera and anthrax could also be killed by radium. fileciteturn191file0L234-L241

10. Marie Curie's Humanity

Marie and Pierre could have patented their discovery and become wealthy, but Marie refused to do so and gave the discovery freely to the world for proper use. When asked why she did not make money from her discoveries, she said that she was working for science and that radium belonged to the people, not to her. fileciteturn191file0L242-L256

Conclusion: In the framework of the lesson, this decision shows that Marie valued scientific progress and public benefit more than personal wealth.

11. Pierre Curie's Death and Marie's Later Life

In 1906 Pierre was knocked down and killed by a horse-drawn wagon. Marie was deeply distressed by his death. In 1911 she received the Nobel Prize for Chemistry. In 1934 her health failed and she died in July of that year. fileciteturn191file0L248-L260

25. Exercise 10 — Expansion of the Maxim

“There is no short-cut to success.”

Success rarely comes instantly. Behind every meaningful achievement there are usually patience, practice, discipline and continuous effort. A person may have talent, but talent becomes useful only when it is developed through hard work.

The life of Madame Curie is a strong example. She studied despite poverty and hunger, worked under difficult conditions and continued her research for years. She and Pierre Curie faced financial hardship and demanding laboratory conditions before their work produced radium. Her success therefore was not the result of an easy path. It was the result of determination, sacrifice and endurance.

Students can apply the same principle to education, sports, careers and other goals. Regular practice and learning from failure gradually produce improvement. Shortcuts may sometimes save time in small tasks, but lasting achievement requires genuine effort and perseverance.

Therefore, the best way to reach a meaningful goal is to work patiently, learn continuously and remain determined. There is no short-cut to success.

The textbook asks for an approximately 20-line expansion with an introduction, examples and conclusion. fileciteturn191file0L409-L411

27. Important Exam Questions

Q1. Why is Marie Curie described as a brave heart?

She continued her scientific work despite poverty, hunger, cold, overwork, health difficulties and personal loss. Her determination and strength of mind were not defeated by unfavourable conditions. fileciteturn191file0L170-L184 fileciteturn191file0L257-L271

Q2. What proves Marie Curie's will power?

Her husband asked her to give up the struggle when her health was affected by overwork, but she resolutely refused. She continued researching the mystery of radium despite poverty and suffering. fileciteturn191file0L176-L184

Q3. Why was the Curies' laboratory difficult to work in?

It was a shabby wooden shed with a leaky skylight and an earthen floor. They worked in extreme heat during summer and freezing cold during winter while processing pitchblende in large quantities. fileciteturn191file0L119-L123 fileciteturn191file0L185-L196

Q4. What makes Marie Curie an exceptional scientist?

Q5. How did Marie Curie show love for humanity?
