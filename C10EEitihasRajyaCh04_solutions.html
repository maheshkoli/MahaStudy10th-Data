Chapter at a Glance

Chapter 4, History of Indian Arts, introduces the meaning of art and explores Indian traditions of visual arts, performing arts, applied art and professional opportunities. It explains how art reflects imagination, emotions, skills, society, history and regional traditions.

The textbook organises the chapter into four sections: What is Art?; Indian Traditions of Visual Arts; Indian Traditions of Performing Arts; and Art, Applied Art and Professional Opportunities.

4.1 What Is Art?

Art is a beautiful creation that expresses human experience, wisdom and emotions. Imagination, sensibility, emotional state and skill are important factors in artistic creation.

Types of art

  • Visual arts (Drik Kala): Art that can be seen, such as painting and sculpture.
  • Performing arts (Lalit or Aangik Kala): Art presented through performance, such as music, dance and theatre.
  • Folk art: A spontaneous tradition that grows from everyday life and collective participation in a community.
  • Classical art: Art practised within an established framework of rules and usually requiring prolonged training.

Style in art

An artist’s individual way of working is their style. When a style is adopted by many artists over a long period, it may become a tradition or art style associated with a particular period and region.

4.2 Indian Traditions of Visual Arts

Painting

Painting is a two-dimensional visual art practised on surfaces such as rock, walls, paper, canvas and earthen pots. The textbook identifies the Bodhisattva mural at Ajanta Caves as a notable example.

Maratha style of painting

  • Developed in the latter half of the 17th century C.E.
  • Includes coloured murals and miniature paintings in manuscripts.
  • Murals can be seen in old wadas at Wai, Menavali and Satara in Maharashtra.
  • Influenced by Rajput and European styles.

Folk painting styles

  • Rock paintings: Found at sites across India; Bhimbetka is a famous World Heritage site. Rock paintings often depict humans, animals and geometric figures. Black, red and white natural colours were used.
  • Warli painting: A folk tradition of Maharashtra. The textbook highlights Jivya Somya Mashe’s contribution to popularising the style and notes that he received the Padma Shri in 2011.
  • Chitrakathi / Pinguli: A storytelling tradition associated with Pinguli village near Kudal in the Konkan region. Pictures painted with natural colours are shown in sequence to narrate stories from the Ramayana or Mahabharata. A story may use 30–50 pictures. The tradition is associated with the Thakar tribal community and is described as needing preservation.

Sculptural art

Sculptures are three-dimensional creations such as images, statues, pots and decorated objects. They can be made from rock, metal or clay. Rock sculptures are carved; metal sculptures may be made using moulds; clay sculptures are shaped by hand or moulds.

  • Examples of folk sculptural traditions: Ganesha idols, Gauri masks, Bailpola bull figurines, wooden memorials, Veergals (memorial stones) and decorated clay storage bins.
  • Classical sculpture: Harappan seals and stone and bronze statues show a very old sculptural tradition. The textbook describes large stone sculpture as developing in the Mauryan period, including Asokan pillars.
  • Indian iconography: The chapter explains that the Gandhara school had Greek and Persian influences, while the Mathura school rose during the Kushana period and helped lay the foundation of Indian iconography.
  • Bronze sculpture: The chapter notes the development of bronze images under Chola patronage between the 9th and 13th centuries C.E.

Architecture and sculpture

Rock-cut caves combine architecture and sculpture through their entrances, carved columns, interiors, images and sometimes surviving wall and ceiling paintings. The chapter says the Ajanta and Verul (Ellora) caves in Maharashtra were declared World Heritage in 1983.

4.3 Indian Traditions of Performing Arts

Folk performing arts

India has varied regional traditions of folk songs, instrumental music, folk dances and folk theatre. The chapter names Maharashtra examples such as Koli Dance, Tarapa Dance, Dashavatar of Konkan, Powada, Keertan and Jagar-Gondhal.

Classical performing arts

The textbook describes Bharatmuni’s Natyashastra as an early text discussing music and theatre. It identifies nine rasas, or moods, used in Indian performing arts:

4.4 Art, Applied Art and Professional Opportunities

Careers connected with art history

  • Art historians can work in journalism.
  • The art market requires experts who can assess an artwork’s value and authenticity.
  • Heritage management and cultural tourism offer professional opportunities.
  • Related fields include museums and archives management, library science and information technology, archaeological research and Indology.

Applied art

Applied art combines artistic creation with practical utility. The chapter gives examples such as industry and advertising, interior design, ornamental objects, stage backdrops, art direction for film and television, book and magazine layouts, calligraphy, greeting and invitation cards, customised stationery, gift objects, architecture, photography, computer-generated still and animated graphics, ornaments, designed earthen pots, cane and bamboo objects, glass objects, textiles and clothing.

The textbook stresses that applied-art production needs planning, management and trained, skilled people. Knowledge of the history of industrial and cultural traditions is included in art-design training. The National Institute of Design in Ahmedabad is cited as a well-known institution.

6. Answer in detail

(1) Write in detail about folk styles of painting.
Folk painting traditions include prehistoric rock paintings and regional forms. Rock paintings at Bhimbetka and other sites show human, animal and geometric figures, using natural black, red and white colours. In Maharashtra, Warli painting and Chitrakathi or Pinguli are important traditions. Chitrakathi uses a sequence of painted pictures to narrate stories from the Ramayana or Mahabharata and is associated with the Thakar community near Kudal. These traditions reflect local life and need preservation.

(2) Explain the characteristics of Islamic architecture in India with examples.
The chapter explains that, under Muslim sultanates, Persian, Central Asian, Arabic and pre-Islamic Indian styles blended to form Indian Islamic architecture. Examples include Qutub Minar near Delhi, the Taj Mahal at Agra and Gol Gumbaz at Bijapur. The Taj Mahal was built by Shah Jahan in memory of Mumtaz Mahal. Gol Gumbaz, built in the 17th century, is noted for its dome and whispering gallery.

(3) What professional opportunities are available in the field of arts?
Opportunities include art journalism, art-market evaluation and authentication, heritage management, cultural tourism, museum and archive management, library science, information technology, archaeological research and Indology. Applied-art careers include advertising, interior design, architecture, photography, film and television art direction, graphic design, calligraphy, publishing layouts, product design, textiles and decorative crafts.
