1. Chapter at a Glance

A chemical reaction changes reactants into products. Chemical equations represent these changes using chemical formulae. Equations must be balanced because the number of atoms of each element—and therefore the total mass—is conserved.

Representing reactions

Reactants → Products; include physical states and reaction conditions when needed.

Reaction types

Combination, decomposition, displacement and double displacement.

Energy and speed

Endothermic/exothermic reactions and factors affecting reaction rate.

Redox and everyday effects

Oxidation, reduction, corrosion of metals and rancidity of food.

2. Important Concepts and Definitions

Physical change and chemical change

  • Physical change: The composition of matter remains the same; often the change can be reversed. Example: ice melting into water.
  • Chemical change: The composition changes and new substance(s) form. Examples: cooking food, rusting and milk turning into curd.

Chemical reaction, reactants and products

A chemical reaction involves breaking some bonds in reactants and forming new bonds to produce substances with different properties.

  • Reactants: Substances present at the start of a reaction, written on the left side of the equation.
  • Products: Substances formed in the reaction, written on the right side.
  • Balanced equation: An equation with the same number of atoms of every element on both sides.

Conventions used in chemical equations

SymbolMeaning
(s), (l), (g), (aq)Solid, liquid, gas and aqueous solution (dissolved in water).
↑Gas is released.
↓An insoluble solid precipitate forms.
Δ above arrowHeat is supplied to the reaction.
Catalyst / temperature above or below arrowReaction condition or catalyst used.
Law of conservation of mass: In a chemical reaction, the total mass of each element in the reactants equals the total mass of that element in the products.

3. Writing and Balancing Chemical Equations

Example: zinc and copper sulphate

CuSO₄(aq) + Zn(s) → ZnSO₄(aq) + Cu(s)

Zinc displaces copper from copper sulphate. The atoms of Cu, S, O and Zn are equal on both sides, so the equation is balanced.

Steps for balancing an equation

  1. Write the correct chemical formulae for all reactants and products.
  2. Count the atoms of each element on both sides.
  3. Balance one element at a time by changing coefficients placed before formulae.
  4. Never change the subscripts inside a chemical formula.
  5. Recount all atoms and write the final balanced equation.

Worked example: sodium hydroxide and sulphuric acid

Unbalanced equation:

NaOH + H₂SO₄ → Na₂SO₄ + H₂O

Put 2 before NaOH to balance sodium, then put 2 before H₂O to balance hydrogen and oxygen.

2NaOH + H₂SO₄ → Na₂SO₄ + 2H₂O

4. Types of Chemical Reactions

1. Combination reaction

Two or more reactants combine to form one product.

CaO(s) + H₂O(l) → Ca(OH)₂(aq) + heat

2. Decomposition reaction

One reactant breaks down into two or more products. Decomposition may be caused by heat, electricity or light.

CaCO₃(s) —Δ→ CaO(s) + CO₂(g)

3. Displacement reaction

A more reactive element displaces a less reactive element from its compound.

CuSO₄(aq) + Fe(s) → FeSO₄(aq) + Cu(s)

4. Double displacement reaction

Ions of two compounds exchange partners, often producing a precipitate.

BaCl₂(aq) + ZnSO₄(aq) → BaSO₄(s)↓ + ZnCl₂(aq)

Endothermic and exothermic reactions

  • Endothermic: Absorbs heat from the surroundings or needs heat supplied. Example: CaCO₃ —Δ→ CaO + CO₂.
  • Exothermic: Releases heat. Example: CaO + H₂O → Ca(OH)₂ + heat.

5. Rate of a Chemical Reaction

Some reactions occur rapidly while others take a long time. The rate can change when reaction conditions change.

FactorEffect described in the chapter
Nature/reactivity of reactantsMore reactive aluminium reacts with dilute HCl faster than zinc in the described comparison.
Particle sizeSmaller particles provide a larger exposed surface area, so the reaction occurs faster.
ConcentrationHigher concentration generally increases reaction rate; concentrated HCl reacts with CaCO₃ faster than dilute HCl.
TemperatureIncreasing temperature generally increases reaction rate.
CatalystA catalyst changes the reaction rate without itself undergoing a permanent chemical change. MnO₂ speeds up decomposition of H₂O₂ and KClO₃.

6. Oxidation and Reduction

  • Oxidation: A reactant combines with oxygen, loses hydrogen, or loses one or more electrons.
  • Reduction: A reactant loses oxygen, gains hydrogen, or gains one or more electrons.
  • Oxidant / oxidizing agent: A substance that brings about oxidation.
  • Reductant / reducing agent: A substance that brings about reduction.
  • Redox reaction: Oxidation and reduction occur simultaneously.

Example: copper oxide and hydrogen

CuO + H₂ → Cu + H₂O

Copper oxide loses oxygen and is reduced to copper. Hydrogen gains oxygen and is oxidized to water. Hydrogen is the reductant; copper oxide is the oxidant in this reaction.

Oxidation-state / electron example from the chapter

Fe²⁺ → Fe³⁺ + e⁻

Iron(II) loses one electron to form iron(III), so it is oxidized. The reverse change, Fe³⁺ + e⁻ → Fe²⁺, is reduction.

7. Corrosion and Rancidity

Corrosion

Corrosion is damage to metals due to reactions with components of the atmosphere. Rusting of iron requires both air and water; salt can make rusting faster. The textbook describes rust as hydrated iron(III) oxide, written as Fe₂O₃·xH₂O.

Anode: Fe(s) → Fe²⁺(aq) + 2e⁻
Cathode: O₂(g) + 4H⁺(aq) + 4e⁻ → 2H₂O(l)

Rancidity

Rancidity is the foul odour and altered taste that develop when oil- or fat-containing food undergoes oxidation. It can be slowed by antioxidants and by storing food in airtight containers.

Question 1. Choose the correct option and explain.

  1. To prevent rusting, a layer of zinc is applied on iron sheets. This process is called galvanizing; the zinc coating protects the iron from air and moisture.
  2. The conversion of ferrous sulphate to ferric sulphate is an oxidation reaction because Fe²⁺ changes to Fe³⁺ by losing an electron.
  3. When electric current is passed through acidulated water, electrolysis of water takes place.
  4. Adding aqueous ZnSO₄ to aqueous BaCl₂ is a double displacement reaction because the ions exchange partners and BaSO₄ precipitates.
ZnSO₄(aq) + BaCl₂(aq) → BaSO₄(s)↓ + ZnCl₂(aq)

Question 2. Write answers to the following.

a. What is the reaction called when oxidation and reduction take place simultaneously? Explain with one example.

Answer: It is called a redox reaction. In CuO + H₂ → Cu + H₂O, CuO loses oxygen and is reduced, while H₂ gains oxygen and is oxidized.

b. How can the rate of decomposition of hydrogen peroxide be increased?

Answer: Add manganese dioxide (MnO₂), which acts as a catalyst.

2H₂O₂(l) —MnO₂→ 2H₂O(l) + O₂(g)

c. Explain reactant and product with examples.

Answer: Reactants are the starting substances; products are the substances formed. In CuSO₄ + Zn → ZnSO₄ + Cu, copper sulphate and zinc are reactants, while zinc sulphate and copper are products.

d. Explain types of reaction with reference to oxygen and hydrogen, with examples.

  • Oxidation: Addition of oxygen or removal of hydrogen. Example: 2Mg + O₂ → 2MgO.
  • Reduction: Removal of oxygen or addition of hydrogen. Example: CuO + H₂ → Cu + H₂O; CuO is reduced.

e. Explain the similarity and difference between adding NaOH to water and adding CaO to water.

Answer: Both processes release heat. Dissolving NaOH in water is a dissolution process; forming calcium hydroxide from CaO and water is a chemical combination reaction that produces a new substance.

Question 3. Explain the following terms with examples.

a. Endothermic reaction: A reaction that absorbs heat. Example: CaCO₃(s) —Δ→ CaO(s) + CO₂(g).

b. Combination reaction: Two or more reactants form one product. Example: CaO + H₂O → Ca(OH)₂ + heat.

c. Balanced equation: An equation with equal numbers of atoms of each element on both sides. Example: 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O.

d. Displacement reaction: A more reactive element displaces a less reactive element from a compound. Example: CuSO₄ + Fe → FeSO₄ + Cu.

Question 4. Give scientific reasons.

a. Lime water turns milky when the gas produced by heating limestone is passed through it.

Answer: Heating limestone (CaCO₃) produces carbon dioxide. It reacts with lime water, Ca(OH)₂, to form insoluble calcium carbonate, which makes the solution milky.

CaCO₃(s) —Δ→ CaO(s) + CO₂(g)
CO₂(g) + Ca(OH)₂(aq) → CaCO₃(s)↓ + H₂O(l)

b. Pieces of Shahabad tile disappear slowly in HCl, but its powder disappears rapidly.

Answer: Powder has a larger exposed surface area than pieces of the same mass, so it reacts faster with HCl.

c. Concentrated sulphuric acid is added slowly to water with constant stirring when preparing dilute acid.

Answer: Dilution releases a large amount of heat. Adding acid slowly to water with stirring helps disperse the heat and reduces the risk of sudden boiling and splashing. Never pour water into concentrated acid.

d. Airtight containers are recommended for storing oil for a long time.

Answer: An airtight container reduces contact with oxygen and slows oxidation, helping prevent rancidity.

Question 5. Explain the chemical reaction shown in the rusting diagram.

Answer: Rusting is an electrochemical process. Iron is oxidized at the anode, releasing electrons. Oxygen is reduced at the cathode in the presence of water. Iron ions ultimately form reddish-brown hydrated iron(III) oxide (rust).

Anode: Fe(s) → Fe²⁺(aq) + 2e⁻
Cathode: O₂(g) + 4H⁺(aq) + 4e⁻ → 2H₂O(l)

The textbook gives rust as Fe₂O₃·xH₂O and describes the cathodic reduction of oxygen and subsequent formation of hydrated iron oxide.

9. Quick Revision Sheet

  • Reactants → Products: Standard arrangement of a chemical equation.
  • Combination: A + B → AB.
  • Decomposition: AB → A + B.
  • Displacement: A + BC → AC + B.
  • Double displacement: AB + CD → AD + CB.
  • Endothermic: Absorbs heat. Exothermic: Releases heat.
  • Oxidation: Gain of oxygen, loss of hydrogen, or loss of electrons.
  • Reduction: Loss of oxygen, gain of hydrogen, or gain of electrons.
  • Redox: Oxidation and reduction occur together.
  • Rusting: Iron corrosion requiring both air and water; salt speeds it up.
  • Rancidity: Oxidation of oil/fat-containing food; limit oxygen exposure and use antioxidants.
Exam tip: When balancing equations, change coefficients only—not the subscripts in chemical formulae. Check every element again before finalizing the answer.
