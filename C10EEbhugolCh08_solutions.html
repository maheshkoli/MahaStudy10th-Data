1. Chapter at a Glance

What this chapter covers

The chapter compares the economies and occupations of India and Brazil. It covers economic sectors, national income and per capita income, agriculture, mining, fishing, industries, exports and imports, balance of trade, and India–Brazil relations through BRICS.

Main idea

Economic activities, natural resources, population, technology, transport and investment influence the economic structure of a country. India and Brazil are described in the textbook as developing countries with mixed economies.

2. Important Definitions

Primary activities

Activities that directly use natural resources, such as agriculture, fishing, animal husbandry and mining.

Secondary activities

Activities that process raw materials or manufacture goods, such as automobile production, rope making and construction.

Tertiary activities

Service activities, such as teaching, banking, television broadcasting, transport and lodging.

GDP

Gross Domestic Product: the monetary value of final goods and services produced within a country during a specified period.

GNI

Gross National Income. Figure 8.1 compares national-income values for Brazil, India and the USA from 1960 to 2016. Use the graph's units and plotted values when answering textbook questions.

Per capita income

Average income per person. The chapter compares per capita income for Brazil, India and the USA in 1960, 1980, 2000 and 2016.

Mixed economy

An economy in which both public/government and private ownership operate.

Exports and imports

Exports are goods sold to other countries; imports are goods bought from other countries.

Balance of trade

The difference between the value of exports and the value of imports.

Favourable balance of trade

When exports exceed imports.

Unfavourable balance of trade

When imports exceed exports.

6. Agriculture in Brazil

General pattern

The chapter says agriculture is a major occupation in Brazil's highlands and coastal areas. Favourable climate and topography allow a variety of crops.

Food crops

Rice and maize are identified as major cereal crops. Maize production is largely concentrated in central Brazil.

Commercial crops

Coffee, cocoa, rubber, soybean and sugarcane are cultivated on a large scale. The chapter states that Brazil is a major exporter of coffee and soybean.

Coffee

The textbook names Minas Gerais and São Paulo as major coffee-growing states. It also notes that coffee farms are called fazendas.

Fruits and animal husbandry

Bananas, pineapples, oranges and other citrus fruits are grown. Cattle, sheep and goats are reared in the southern savannah grasslands, supporting meat and dairy production.

Mineral resources

Why mining is limited in interior areas

The textbook gives inaccessibility, lack of knowledge about potential reserves and dense forests as reasons mining has been limited in interior parts of Brazil.

Mining development

8. Fishing in Brazil and India

Fishing in Brazil

The textbook says Brazil has about 7,400 km of coastline and good fishing grounds off the South Atlantic coast. It attributes good fishing off south-eastern Brazil to the meeting of the warm Brazil Current and cold Falkland Current.

Marine catch in Brazil

The chapter lists swordfish, shrimp, lobsters and sardines among the main catches. It says fishing in the Amazon River is not heavily exploited and occurs on a small scale.

Fishing in India

The chapter describes India as a major producer of marine and inland fish. Fishing supports food supply, employment, nutrition and foreign-exchange earnings.

Marine fishing in India

The textbook mentions coastal fishing from the Kachchh and Malabar coasts to the Coromandel coast, and lists sardines, mackerel, Bombay duck and prawns among important fish.

Inland fishing in India

Freshwater fishing occurs in rivers, canals, irrigation channels, tanks, ponds and lakes. The chapter notes that inland fisheries contribute a substantial share of India's fish production.

Comparison answer

Both countries undertake marine fishing and have extensive coastlines. Brazil's south-eastern fishing grounds are linked in the chapter to the meeting of warm and cold ocean currents, while India has both marine and inland fisheries, including fishing in rivers, ponds and reservoirs.

9. Agriculture and Mining in India

Agriculture

The textbook says agriculture contributes more to India's GDP and engages a larger share of the population than it does in Brazil. It attributes India's agricultural base to extensive level plains, rich soils, cultivable land, climatic variety and a long growing season.

Food crops

Rice, wheat, maize, sorghum and millets are listed as major food crops.

Plantations and cash crops

Tea, coffee and rubber plantations, along with sugarcane, cotton and jute, are mentioned in the chapter.

Fruits and vegetables

India is described as a major producer of a variety of fruits and vegetables.

Mining regions

The chapter identifies the Chhota Nagpur Plateau as a major mineral storehouse. It mentions coal in Korba (Chhattisgarh) and eastern Maharashtra; oil wells at Digboi (Assam), Mumbai High, Kalol and Koyali (Gujarat); and mineral oil and natural gas near the mouth of the Godavari River.

Other minerals

The textbook mentions marble in Rajasthan and Cuddapah in Andhra Pradesh.

10. Industries in Brazil and India

Industries in Brazil

The chapter lists iron and steel, automobile assembly, petroleum processing, chemicals and cement. It also mentions food processing, sugar, cotton textiles, silk and woollen industries.

Industrial location in Brazil

The textbook says most large industry is concentrated in southern and south-eastern Brazil. It describes the north-east as traditionally poorer but beginning to attract new investment.

Industries in India

The textbook explains that India's industrial distribution is uneven because raw materials, power, enterprises, financial resources and other conditions are unevenly distributed.

Heavy and metallurgical industries

The chapter identifies Jharkhand, Odisha, adjoining Chhattisgarh and Madhya Pradesh, parts of Rajasthan, Karnataka and Tamil Nadu as important mineral-reserve areas. It links the north-eastern part of the peninsula with a high concentration of heavy metallurgical industries.

Agro-based industries

Cotton, jute and sugar industries are concentrated near areas producing their raw materials.

Forest-based industries

Paper, plywood, matches, resins and lac industries are associated with forest areas.

Other industrial examples

The chapter mentions coir, copra and fish-canning industries along coastal Kerala; refineries at Koyali, Digboi, Noonmati, Bongaigaon, Mathura and Barauni; and salt production in Gujarat, Rajasthan and Tamil Nadu.

11. Trade and Balance of Trade

Formula

Balance of trade = value of exports − value of imports.

Favourable balance

Exports are greater than imports; the balance is positive.

Unfavourable balance

Imports are greater than exports; the balance is negative.

Table data in the textbook

Export, import and balance-of-trade questions use the values in the textbook table for 2009–10 through 2012–13. Always compare exports and imports for the same country and year.

Brazil's exports

The chapter lists iron ore, coffee, cocoa, cotton, sugar, tobacco, oranges and bananas among Brazil's exports.

Brazil's imports

The chapter lists machinery, chemical products, fertilisers, wheat, heavy vehicles, mineral oil and lubricants among imports.

India's exports

The textbook lists tea, mangoes, coffee, spices, leather and leather goods, iron ore, cotton textiles and silk textiles.

India's imports

The chapter lists petroleum, machines, pearls and precious stones, gold and silver, paper and medicines.

Trading partners

Correct option: Developing.

The chapter gives inaccessibility, lack of knowledge about potential reserves and dense forests as reasons limiting mining in interior parts.

14. Worked Calculations and Graph Skills

Balance of trade example

If exports are 200 units and imports are 150 units, balance of trade = 200 − 150 = +50 units, a favourable balance. If exports are 120 and imports are 170, the balance is 120 − 170 = −50 units, an unfavourable balance.

Per capita income concept

Per capita income = national income ÷ population. This explains why a country with a higher total national income may still have a lower average income per person if its population is much larger.

Graph analysis method

1. Read the title and unit. 2. Identify the country, year and legend. 3. Compare the relevant bars or lines. 4. Quote values only when readable. 5. State the trend and what it suggests.

Map analysis method

Read the map legend first. Match symbols to crops, minerals, animal husbandry or fisheries. Describe the regions shown without assuming the map provides exact production amounts.
