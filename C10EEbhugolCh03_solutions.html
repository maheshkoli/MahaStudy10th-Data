1. Chapter at a Glance

Main idea

This chapter compares the physical features (physiography) and drainage systems of India and Brazil. The textbook uses physical maps, elevation colours and river maps to examine altitude, slope, plateaus, mountains, plains and river basins.

India: five major physiographic divisions

The Himalayas; North Indian Plains; the Peninsula; Coastal Plains; and Island groups.

Brazil: physiographic divisions

The Highlands; the Great Escarpment; the Coastal region; the Plains; and Island groups.

Drainage

The textbook classifies Indian rivers into Himalayan and Peninsular rivers. It describes the Amazon Basin, Paraguay–Parana system and Sao Francisco basin among Brazil’s major drainage systems.

2. India — Physiography

The Himalayas

Young fold mountains extending eastward from the Pamir Knot. In India, they extend from Jammu and Kashmir to Arunachal Pradesh. The textbook lists the Siwaliks, Lesser Himalayas, Greater Himalayas (Himadri) and Trans-Himalayan ranges from south to north; these ranges are described as young to old respectively.

North Indian Plains

A mostly flat, low-lying region between the Himalayas in the north and the Peninsula in the south, extending from Rajasthan and Punjab in the west to Assam in the east. The Ganga Plains lie east of the Aravalis and slope eastward. The Ganga–Brahmaputra delta is called the Sunderbans. The western part includes the Thar Desert or Marusthali.

The Peninsula

The region south of the North Indian Plains, tapering towards the Indian Ocean. It includes plateaus and hill ranges such as the Aravalis, Vindhyas, Satpudas, Western Ghats and Eastern Ghats.

Coastal Plains

The textbook gives India’s coastline as approximately 7,500 km. The western coast borders the Arabian Sea; it is generally rocky and narrow, and short, swift rivers form estuaries. The eastern coast borders the Bay of Bengal; river deposition creates deltas.

Island groups

Lakshadweep lies in the Arabian Sea and most of its islands are atolls. The Andaman and Nicobar Islands lie in the Bay of Bengal. The Andaman group is mainly volcanic; Barren Island is described as having India’s only active volcano.

3. Brazil — Physiography

The Highlands

The southern part of Brazil is occupied by an extensive plateau known as the Brazilian Highlands, Brazilian Shield or Brazilian Plateau. Together, the Brazilian and Guyana Highlands form the core of the South American continent.

Guyana Highlands and Pico de Neblina

The main part of the Guyana Highlands lies in Venezuela and extends to French Guiana. In Brazil, the textbook names Roraima, Pará and Amapá. Pico de Neblina is given as Brazil’s highest peak at 3,014 m, on the Brazil–Venezuela border.

Altitude and slope

The regions east and south of the Brazilian Highlands exceed 1,000 m in altitude. Other parts are generally 500–1,000 m. The highlands slope gradually northward; tributaries of the Amazon form rapids and waterfalls in this region.

The Great Escarpment

A steep eastern edge of the Brazilian Highlands. The textbook says it acts as a barrier to the Southeast Trade winds and creates a rain-shadow area in the northeastern highlands, called the Drought Quadrilateral.

Coastal region

Brazil has a coastline of about 7,400 km. The northern coast is low-lying and includes river mouths, Marajo Island and bays. The eastern coast has many smaller rivers, beaches, sand dunes and, in some areas, coral reefs and atoll islands.

Plains

Brazil’s plains are concentrated in the Amazon Basin in the north and the Paraguay–Parana source region in the southwest. The Amazon plains lie between the Guyana and Brazilian Highlands, are generally low-lying, and are largely covered by tropical rainforests. Pantanal is a major wetland in the southwest.

4. Drainage of India

Himalayan rivers

Most major Himalayan rivers originate from glaciers. Their flow continues through the year, and discharge increases when glaciers melt and during monsoon rains. The textbook highlights the Sindhu (Indus) and Ganga river systems.

Sindhu (Indus) system

Jhelum, Chenab, Ravi and Satluj drain the western Himalayas and flow roughly parallel to one another. Satluj originates near Lake Manasarovar. The Sindhu flows through Pakistan and enters the Arabian Sea.

Ganga system

The Ganga originates from the Gangotri glacier and flows eastward. Yamuna, originating at Yamunotri, is a major tributary. The textbook also describes the Brahmaputra as Tsang Po in Tibet, Dihang after crossing the Himalayas, and Brahmaputra as it flows eastward in India. Ganga and Brahmaputra join in Bangladesh and form a large delta.

Peninsular rivers

These rivers are rain-fed and seasonal. The Western Ghats form a major water divide. Peninsular rivers are divided into east-flowing rivers that meet the Bay of Bengal and west-flowing rivers that meet the Arabian Sea.

West-flowing rivers

Rivers between the Western Ghats and the Arabian Sea are generally short and swift. The textbook names Tapi, Narmada, Mahi, Sabarmati and Luni among the west-flowing systems. Tapi and Narmada flow through rift valleys.

East-flowing rivers

Important systems entering the Bay of Bengal include Mahanadi, Godavari, Krishna and Kaveri. Godavari is described as India’s second-largest river system by catchment area. Bhima and Tungabhadra are major tributaries of Krishna.

5. Drainage of Brazil

Amazon Basin

The Amazon collects headwaters from the eastern slopes of the Andes in Peru. The textbook gives its water discharge as about 2 lakh cubic metres per second. Its high discharge carries away sediment, so a typical delta is absent at its mouth; islands occur around the mouth. Much of the river is navigable.

Paraguay–Parana system

These rivers lie in southwestern Brazil and form part of the catchment of the River Plata in Argentina. The Paraguay, Parana and Uruguay collect headwaters from the southern part of the highlands.

Sao Francisco

The entire basin lies within Brazil, in the eastern part of the highlands. The river flows north over the plateau for about 1,000 km, then turns east to enter the coastal strip and the Atlantic Ocean. The textbook says its downstream section is navigable for about 250 km.

Coastal rivers

Brazil has many short coastal rivers. The textbook names Parnaíba and Itapecuru as rivers flowing north to the North Atlantic Ocean, and mentions other rivers that reach the Atlantic along the escarpment.

6. Key Terms

Physiography

The physical features and relief of a region.

Drainage basin / catchment area

The area drained by a river and its tributaries.

Water divide

An elevated area, such as a mountain range, separating neighbouring river basins.

Perennial river

A river that flows throughout the year.

Seasonal river

A river whose flow depends strongly on seasonal rainfall.

Escarpment

A long, steep slope, often forming the edge of a plateau.

Rain-shadow area

An area receiving less rainfall because hills or mountains block rain-bearing winds.

Delta

A depositional landform formed at a river mouth. The textbook notes that a typical delta is absent at the Amazon mouth because of its huge discharge.

Atoll

A ring-shaped coral island or reef; the textbook identifies most Lakshadweep islands as atolls.

The Western Ghats, also called the Sahyadris.

Characteristics of the Western Ghats

They form a major water divide; many rivers originate there. The western coast is narrow and rocky, and west-flowing rivers are short and swift.

Compare the Eastern and Western Ghats.

The Western Ghats are generally more continuous and higher. The Eastern Ghats are discontinuous and lower, with river valleys interrupting them.

Why are the Western Ghats called a water divide?

They separate rivers flowing west to the Arabian Sea from rivers flowing east to the Bay of Bengal.

12. Textbook Exercise — Q3: Write Notes

(a) Amazon River basin

The Amazon receives headwaters from the eastern slopes of the Andes in Peru. It has an exceptionally large discharge (about 2 lakh m³/s in the textbook), a wide low-lying basin between the Guyana and Brazilian Highlands, and extensive tropical rainforest. The high volume of water carries sediment away, so a typical delta is absent at the mouth. Much of the river is navigable.

(b) Himalayas

The Himalayas are young fold mountains extending from the Pamir Knot eastward. In India they extend from Jammu and Kashmir to Arunachal Pradesh. The textbook lists parallel ranges: Siwaliks, Lesser Himalayas, Greater Himalayas (Himadri) and Trans-Himalayan ranges. Many major rivers originate in Himalayan glaciers and are perennial.

(c) The coasts of Brazil

Brazil’s coastline is about 7,400 km. The northern coast is low-lying and has many river mouths, including the Amazon, as well as Marajo Island and bays. The eastern coast has many smaller rivers, beaches, sand dunes and, in some areas, coral reefs and atoll islands.

(d) The Indian Peninsula

The Peninsula lies south of the North Indian Plains and tapers towards the Indian Ocean. It includes plateaus and hill ranges, including the Aravalis, Vindhyas, Satpudas, Western Ghats and Eastern Ghats. The Western Ghats act as a major water divide.

(e) The Great Escarpment

It marks the steep eastern edge of the Brazilian Highlands. The textbook gives its altitude as about 790 m in the region discussed and says the escarpment blocks Southeast Trade winds, creating a rain-shadow area called the Drought Quadrilateral.

13. Textbook Exercise — Q4: Give Geographical Reasons

(a) There are no west-flowing rivers in Brazil.

The textbook describes the Brazilian Highlands as sloping steeply towards the east in the escarpment region. Rivers from the southern slopes flow towards Argentina, and rivers from the terminal portions of the highlands flow northwards or towards the Atlantic. The chapter’s intended point is the overall slope and drainage arrangement; it does not give a detailed standalone explanation for the wording of this statement.

(b) There are dissimilarities between the eastern and western coasts of India.

The western coast is generally rocky and narrow, with short, swift rivers that form estuaries. The eastern coast is shaped by deposition from rivers; its gentler slope allows sediment to accumulate, so deltas are common.

(c) There are fewer natural ports on the eastern coast of India.

The textbook describes the eastern coast as depositional, with river sediments forming deltas. Such depositional features make the coast different from the rocky western coast, where estuaries occur. This helps explain the textbook’s comparison.

(d) Compared with the Amazon, pollution in the Ganga will affect human life greatly.

The Ganga flows through densely populated and intensively used regions of India, so pollution can directly affect many people who depend on its water. The Amazon basin, as described in the textbook, includes extensive forested and relatively inaccessible areas. This is an explanatory inference; the chapter does not provide pollution measurements.
