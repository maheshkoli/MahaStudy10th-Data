1. Chapter at a Glance

This chapter studies heat transfer during temperature changes and changes of state. It explains why temperature can remain constant while a substance melts or boils, why water behaves unusually between 0°C and 4°C, how humidity and dew point are defined, and how specific heat capacity is measured.

Latent heat

Energy absorbed or released during a change of state at constant temperature.

Water’s unusual behaviour

Water has minimum volume and maximum density at 4°C.

Humidity

Water vapour in air, saturation and dew-point temperature.

Specific heat

Heat needed to change the temperature of unit mass by one degree.

2. Latent Heat

During a change of state, a substance may absorb or release heat while its temperature remains constant. This energy is used to change the arrangement and bonding of particles rather than increase their temperature.

Latent heat of fusion

The heat energy absorbed at constant temperature while a solid changes into a liquid is called latent heat of fusion. The specific latent heat of fusion is the heat required to convert unit mass of a solid into liquid at its melting point.

Latent heat of vaporization

The heat energy absorbed at constant temperature while a liquid changes into gas is called latent heat of vaporization. The specific latent heat of vaporization is the heat required to convert unit mass of liquid into gas at its boiling point.

3. Regelation

Regelation is the phenomenon in which ice melts under applied pressure and refreezes when that pressure is removed. Pressure lowers the melting point of ice; when pressure is removed, the melting point returns towards 0°C and the water refreezes.

Wire-through-ice experiment

A weighted thin wire is placed across an ice slab. The wire gradually passes through the slab, but the ice above it joins again. Pressure under the wire lowers the melting point, melting the ice locally. Once the wire passes, the pressure is removed and the water refreezes.

The textbook introduces this phenomenon using an ice slab, a thin wire and equal weights (Figure 5.3, page 64).

4. Anomalous Behaviour of Water

Most liquids expand when heated and contract when cooled. Water behaves differently between 0°C and 4°C: when heated from 0°C to 4°C, it contracts. Its volume is minimum and its density is maximum at 4°C. Above 4°C, water expands as it is heated.

Hope’s apparatus

The textbook describes a cylindrical container of water with a flat outer bowl containing a freezing mixture. Thermometers measure temperatures above (T₂) and below (T₁) the bowl. As the water cools, the denser water at 4°C moves downwards. Water cooled below 4°C becomes less dense and rises, eventually cooling the upper part towards 0°C. The lower part stays near 4°C for a time.

Why this matters

  • Aquatic life: In winter, water near 4°C remains beneath the colder water and ice at the surface. The ice layer also reduces heat loss, helping aquatic organisms survive.
  • Cracked rocks and pipes: Water entering cracks expands when it freezes, exerting pressure that can split rocks and damage water pipes.

5. Dew Point and Humidity

Humidity refers to moisture (water vapour) present in the atmosphere. For a given temperature, air can contain a limited amount of water vapour before becoming saturated. Cooler air requires less water vapour to reach saturation than warmer air.

  • Saturated air: Air containing the maximum possible water vapour at that temperature.
  • Unsaturated air: Air containing less than the maximum possible water vapour at that temperature.
  • Dew-point temperature: The temperature at which unsaturated air becomes saturated when cooled.
  • Absolute humidity: Mass of water vapour per unit volume of air; commonly expressed in kg/m³.
  • Relative humidity: The ratio of actual water-vapour content to the amount needed to saturate the air at the same temperature, expressed as a percentage.
Relative humidity (%) = (Actual water-vapour content ÷ Saturation water-vapour content) × 100

At the dew point, relative humidity is 100%. The textbook notes that relative humidity above 60% generally feels humid, while below 60% generally feels dry.

Why droplets form on a cold bottle

Water vapour in the surrounding air cools near the cold bottle surface. When the air reaches its dew point, excess vapour condenses into liquid droplets. Dew forms in a similar way on grass and other cool surfaces.

6. Specific Heat Capacity

Specific heat capacity is the heat energy required to raise the temperature of unit mass of a substance by 1°C. It is denoted by c.

Q = m × c × ΔT

Here, Q is heat energy, m is mass, c is specific heat capacity and ΔT is the change in temperature. The SI unit is J/(kg·°C) or J/(kg·K); the textbook also lists cal/(g·°C).

Three-sphere experiment

Equal-mass spheres of iron, copper and lead are heated in boiling water and then placed on wax. The iron sphere sinks deepest into the wax, the lead sphere least, and copper is intermediate. This shows that equal masses of different substances absorb and release different amounts of heat for the same temperature change.

7. Principle of Heat Exchange and Calorimeter

When a hot object and a cold object are placed in an isolated system, heat transfers from the hot object to the cold object until they reach the same temperature.

Heat lost by hot object = Heat gained by cold object

In the mixing method, a calorimeter is used. For a hot solid placed in water in a calorimeter:

Heat lost by solid = Heat gained by water + Heat gained by calorimeter

The heat for a temperature change is calculated using Q = mcΔT. Use consistent units for mass, specific heat and temperature change.

Units of heat

  • SI unit: joule (J).
  • CGS unit: calorie (cal).
  • 1 kcal = 1000 cal.
  • 1 cal = 4.18 J.

The textbook defines one calorie using the heat required to raise 1 g of water from 14.5°C to 15.5°C, because the specific heat of water varies slightly with temperature.

8. Solved Examples

Example 1 — Heat needed to warm water

Given: m = 5 kg, c = 1 kcal/(kg·°C), initial temperature = 20°C, final temperature = 100°C.

ΔT = 100 − 20 = 80°C
Q = mcΔT = 5 × 1 × 80 = 400 kcal

Answer: 400 kcal.

Example 2 — Final temperature in a calorimeter

Given: Copper sphere 100 g at 100°C; water 195 g at 20°C; copper calorimeter 50 g at 20°C. Specific heat of copper and calorimeter = 0.1 cal/(g·°C), water = 1 cal/(g·°C).

Heat lost by copper = 100 × 0.1 × (100 − T) = 10(100 − T)
Heat gained by water = 195(T − 20)
Heat gained by calorimeter = 50 × 0.1(T − 20) = 5(T − 20)
10(100 − T) = 195(T − 20) + 5(T − 20)
1000 − 10T = 200T − 4000 ⇒ 5000 = 210T
T = 5000/210 ≈ 23.8°C

Answer: approximately 23.8°C.

Example 3 — Ice melted by steam

Given in the textbook: 80 g steam at 97°C; ice at 0°C; latent heat of fusion = 80 cal/g; latent heat of vaporization = 540 cal/g; specific heat of water = 1 cal/(g·°C).

Heat released on condensation = 80 × 540 = 43,200 cal
Heat released as water cools to 0°C = 80 × 1 × 97 = 7,760 cal
Total heat = 43,200 + 7,760 = 50,960 cal
Mass of ice melted = 50,960 ÷ 80 = 637 g

Answer according to the textbook calculation: 637 g of ice.

The example’s stated steam temperature of 97°C is reproduced from the source rather than silently altered.

9. Textbook Exercise Solutions

Q1. Fill in the blanks and rewrite the sentences.

Q2. Observe the graph. Discuss the behaviour of water from 0°C and name it.

Answer: As water is heated from 0°C to 4°C, its volume decreases. Above 4°C, its volume increases with temperature. Most substances expand when heated. Water’s unusual behaviour between 0°C and 4°C is called the anomalous behaviour of water.

Q3. What is specific heat capacity? Describe the experiment proving that substances have different specific heat capacities.

Answer: Specific heat capacity is the heat required to raise the temperature of unit mass of a substance by 1°C. Take equal-mass iron, copper and lead spheres and heat them in boiling water until all reach 100°C. Place them on a wax slab and compare how far they sink. The iron sphere sinks deepest, copper to an intermediate depth and lead least. This demonstrates that the substances release different amounts of heat for the same mass and temperature change.

Q4. Which temperature interval is chosen when defining the unit of heat? Why?

Answer: The interval from 14.5°C to 15.5°C is chosen for defining one calorie. The specific heat of water varies slightly with temperature, so a specified temperature interval gives a standard definition.

Q5. Explain the temperature-versus-time graph.

Answer: AB: ice and water coexist while ice melts at 0°C; the supplied heat is latent heat of fusion. BC: the water temperature rises from 0°C to 100°C. The horizontal boiling segment: water changes into steam at 100°C while the temperature remains constant; the supplied heat is latent heat of vaporization.

Q6. Explain the following.

a. How does anomalous behaviour of water preserve aquatic life in cold climates?
Answer: Water at 4°C has maximum density and sinks. Colder water remains near the surface and can freeze there. The surface ice layer insulates the water below, allowing aquatic life to survive.

b. Relate droplets on a cold bottle to dew formation.
Answer: Air near the cold surface cools to its dew point. It becomes saturated and excess water vapour condenses into droplets. Dew forms by the same process on cool grass and leaves.

c. Why do rocks crack in winter in cold regions?
Answer: Water enters cracks in rocks and expands when it freezes. The expansion creates pressure on the sides of the cracks and may split the rock.

Q7. Answer the following.

a. What is latent heat? What happens when latent heat is given off?
Answer: Latent heat is energy absorbed or released during a change of state at constant temperature. When it is given off, a substance can change to a lower-energy state, such as gas to liquid or liquid to solid.

b. Which principle is used to measure specific heat capacity?
Answer: The principle of heat exchange, using the mixing method and a calorimeter.

c. Explain the role of latent heat in change of state.
Answer: Latent heat is absorbed or released to change the state of a substance without changing its temperature during the transition.

d. How can you determine whether air is saturated with water vapour?
Answer: Cool the air. The temperature at which it becomes saturated is the dew point; further cooling causes excess vapour to condense into droplets.

Q8. Read the paragraph on heat exchange and answer.

i. Heat is transferred from where to where?
From the hot object to the cold object.

ii. Which principle is described?
The principle of heat exchange.

iii. State the principle briefly.
In an isolated system, heat lost by the hot object equals heat gained by the cold object.

iv. Which property can be measured using this principle?
Specific heat capacity.

Q9(a). Equal heat is given to two 1 g objects A and B. Their temperature rises are 3°C and 5°C. Which has greater specific heat, and by what factor?

Q = mcΔT. Since Q and m are equal, c is inversely proportional to ΔT.
cA/cB = ΔTB/ΔTA = 5/3

Answer: Object A; its specific heat is 5/3 times that of B.

Q9(b). Liquid ammonia is used to make 2 kg of ice at 0°C from water at 20°C. Find the mass of ammonia to evaporate.

Use the textbook values: latent heat of vaporization of ammonia = 341 cal/g; latent heat of fusion of ice = 80 cal/g; specific heat of water = 1 cal/(g·°C).

Heat removed to cool 2000 g water from 20°C to 0°C = 2000 × 1 × 20 = 40,000 cal
Heat removed to freeze 2000 g water = 2000 × 80 = 160,000 cal
Total heat removed = 200,000 cal
Ammonia mass = 200,000 ÷ 341 ≈ 586.5 g

Answer: approximately 586.4 g, matching the textbook’s printed answer (rounding).

Q9(c). An insulated pot contains 150 g ice at 0°C. How much steam at 100°C must be mixed with it to obtain water at 50°C?

Use latent heat of fusion = 80 cal/g, latent heat of vaporization = 540 cal/g and specific heat of water = 1 cal/(g·°C).

Heat needed to melt ice = 150 × 80 = 12,000 cal
Heat needed to warm melted ice from 0°C to 50°C = 150 × 1 × 50 = 7,500 cal
Total heat required = 19,500 cal

Each gram of steam releases 540 cal on condensing and 50 cal on cooling from 100°C to 50°C:

Heat released per gram = 540 + 50 = 590 cal/g
Steam mass = 19,500 ÷ 590 ≈ 33.1 g

Answer: approximately 33 g, matching the textbook.

Q9(d). A calorimeter of mass 100 g and specific heat 0.1 kcal/(kg·°C) contains 250 g liquid at 30°C with specific heat 0.4 kcal/(kg·°C). Ten grams of ice at 0°C is added. Find the final temperature.

Use the textbook’s printed answer and the heat-exchange method. The ice first melts, then the resulting water warms to the final temperature.

Heat released by liquid + calorimeter = heat needed to melt ice + heat gained by melted ice-water

10. Quick Revision Sheet

  • Latent heat: Energy absorbed or released during phase change at constant temperature.
  • Fusion: Solid → liquid; heat is absorbed.
  • Freezing: Liquid → solid; heat is released.
  • Vaporization: Liquid → gas; heat is absorbed.
  • Regelation: Pressure-induced melting of ice followed by refreezing when pressure is removed.
  • Anomalous behaviour: Water contracts on heating from 0°C to 4°C; density is maximum at 4°C.
  • Dew point: Temperature at which air becomes saturated on cooling.
  • Relative humidity: Actual vapour content ÷ saturation vapour content × 100%.
  • Specific heat: Q = mcΔT.
  • Heat exchange: Heat lost = heat gained in an isolated system.
  • Conversions: 1 kcal = 1000 cal; 1 cal = 4.18 J.
Exam tip: For heat calculations, keep mass and specific-heat units consistent. Identify whether a process includes a temperature change (Q = mcΔT), a change of state (Q = mL), or both.
